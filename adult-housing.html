Housing for an adult:
vouchers, waiting lists, and buying a place

Where someone lives determines almost everything else about their life, and it is the hardest thing to arrange. Subsidized housing has waiting lists measured in years. Family-owned solutions are possible and carry rules that can quietly cost someone their benefits. Both are worth understanding long before anyone needs to move.

Two rules that catch nearly everyone

Get on waiting lists years early. Housing lists in many areas are five to ten years, and some open only occasionally for a few days. Your position depends entirely on when you applied. Apply to several, in several towns, whether or not you want to move yet. You can decline an offer.

Free housing reduces SSI. If someone else pays for a person's shelter or food, Social Security counts it as income and reduces the payment, potentially by around a third. A generous arrangement — letting an adult live rent-free in a family property, can cost them money and complicate their Medicaid. Structure it properly before you do it, not after.

Subsidized housing, in plain terms

Housing Choice Vouchers

Often still called Section 8. The tenant finds a private rental; the voucher pays a portion directly to the landlord, and the tenant pays roughly a third of their income.

  • Administered by local public housing authorities, each with its own list
  • Apply to every authority you can reach, not just your own town
  • Portable in many cases, so a voucher issued in one place can move
  • Lists often closed, and open by lottery for short windows — set alerts

Most valuable thing hereBecause it follows the person rather than the building, it gives real choice about where to live.

Vouchers set aside for disability

Several categories exist specifically for non-elderly people with disabilities, sometimes with shorter lists than the general pool.

  • Ask each housing authority directly what disability-specific vouchers they administer
  • Some are tied to leaving or avoiding an institution
  • Some are linked to state agency referral rather than open application

Ask explicitlyThese are rarely advertised. Phone and ask what exists rather than relying on the website.

Project-based and supportive housing

The subsidy is attached to a specific building rather than to the person. Some developments are built specifically for people with disabilities, sometimes with support services attached.

  • Often shorter waits than general vouchers
  • You must stay in that unit to keep the subsidy
  • Federal programs fund supportive housing specifically for people with disabilities

Trade-offFaster and more supported, but less freedom to move.

Public housing and tax-credit properties

Public housing is owned by the authority; tax-credit properties are privately owned with income-restricted rents.

  • Tax-credit buildings have their own applications and their own lists
  • Rents are set by income band rather than by a voucher
  • Some units are designed accessible and reserved as such

Search termAsk about income-restricted or affordable units, not just Section 8.

Waiver-funded residential settings

Group homes, shared living and supported living funded through Medicaid rather than housing programs. Housing and support arrive together.

  • Accessed through the state disability agency, not a housing authority
  • Its own waiting list, entirely separate from housing lists
  • Get on both. They are different lists for different things

Common mistakeAssuming the disability agency list covers housing. It usually doesn't.

Your rights as a tenant

Fair housing law protects people with disabilities in nearly all private and public housing.

  • Reasonable accommodation — a change in rules or policy, such as allowing an assistance animal in a no-pets building, or accepting a co-signer
  • Reasonable modification — a physical change such as a ramp or grab bars
  • Refusing to rent because of disability is unlawful
  • Complaints go to HUD or your state fair housing agency, and there are deadlines

Put requests in writingDated, specific, and with a doctor's letter where the need isn't obvious.

Buying or providing a home yourself

Many families consider buying a condo, a small house, or a two-family and having the adult live there. It can work extremely well. It also has rules that determine whether it helps or harms.

The in-kind support rule, which is the whole problem

Social Security counts free or below-cost housing and food as income. If a family provides shelter at no charge, SSI is typically reduced — historically by up to about a third of the payment, and the arrangement has to be reported.

So the generous version, where a parent simply buys a flat and lets their adult child live there for nothing, is often the version that costs the person money and complicates their eligibility. The fix is structure, not generosity.

Ways families make it work

  • The person pays a fair rent from their own income, under a genuine written lease. If they are paying their share of shelter costs, there is no in-kind support to count. This is the cleanest arrangement and the one most attorneys start from.
  • A trust owns the property and the person lives there. This can work well, but how the trust pays for shelter matters enormously to the SSI calculation — trust distributions for housing can themselves be counted. Structure it with a special needs attorney, before purchase.
  • An ABLE account pays housing costs. Housing is a qualified expense, though the timing of withdrawals matters. This is one of the more flexible tools available and it is under-used.
  • A shared arrangement — the person rents a room in a family-owned property alongside other tenants, at market rate for the room.
  • A homeownership voucher. Some housing authorities allow a voucher to be used toward mortgage costs rather than rent, for buyers who meet the criteria. Rare, and fair to ask about.
  • Buying with siblings or other families, sometimes through a nonprofit, to create a small shared setting. Several parent-founded models exist and are worth researching in your region.

Before you buy anything

Ask the special needs attorney first, not the realtor. Who should own it — you, a trust, the person, a company? What rent must be charged, and to whom? How does this interact with SSI, Medicaid, and any waiver? What happens to the property when you die, and does that create a Medicaid problem?

Also worth asking: can a voucher be used to rent from a relative? The general rule is no, with a possible exception as a disability accommodation, so do not assume a family-owned unit can be voucher-funded without checking with that specific housing authority.

An hour of advice before purchase is dramatically cheaper than restructuring afterward, and restructuring can itself trigger benefit problems.

Making a place actually work

  • Housing and support are two separate things. A flat with no support hours is not a plan. Line up both, and confirm the support is funded before anyone signs a lease.
  • Think about who is nearby. Isolation is the most common failure of independent living. Proximity to family, transport, and something to walk to matters more than the unit itself.
  • Check the practical geography — bus route, shop, pharmacy, the program they attend, and how they get to all of it without a car.
  • Plan for nights and emergencies. Who comes at 2am? What happens if a support worker doesn't turn up? Write it down before it is tested.
  • Ask about safety modifications early — stove shut-offs, water temperature limits, door alarms, and monitoring the person agrees to. Consent matters here; see safety.
  • Expect a transition period. Moving is destabilizing for anyone, and more so for someone who relies on routine. Plan extra support for the first months rather than treating early difficulty as evidence it isn't working.
  • Keep the door open both ways. Moving home for a while is not a failure, and arrangements that allow it tend to last longer than arrangements that treat it as defeat.

Before you close this

This week: find your local housing authority's website and see whether their list is open. If it is, apply today. It costs nothing and you can decline whatever you are offered. If it is closed, find out how you will be told when it opens, and set a reminder to check every few months.

People who applied years before they needed anything are the ones with choices. That is almost the whole of it.

Nothing here is legal, financial, or housing advice. Housing programs, benefit rules, and their interaction vary by state and by individual housing authority, and change often. Consult a special needs planning attorney before buying property or structuring any housing arrangement for someone receiving benefits.
