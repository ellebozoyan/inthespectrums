Occupational therapy:
the whole day is the treatment

The name confuses almost everyone. "Occupation" here doesn't mean a job — it means the things that occupy a person's day. For a child that's dressing, eating, playing, writing, managing a body in a loud room, and getting to sleep. OT is the discipline that works on all of it, which is why it's often the hardest therapy to explain and the one families end up relying on most.

Reading this as a professional

Written to be readable by families and by colleagues in other disciplines. The evidence section sorts common approaches into three bands, which may be useful when a family arrives asking about something they read online.

The section on what strong OT looks like is written so families can recognize it — and is worth a skim if you're ever explaining your own practice model to a parent who has had a poor experience elsewhere.

What an occupational therapist is actually looking at

Not a skill in isolation — a day, and every point in it where a child gets stuck, and what would have to change for them to take part.

WakingArousal, transition out of sleep, tolerating the shift
DressingFasteners, sequencing, seams and textures, standing balance
BreakfastUtensils, chewing, sitting, noticing hunger
Getting thereCar seats, transitions, carrying, coats and shoes
ClassroomPosture, handwriting, attention, noise, staying regulated
RecessPlayground equipment, motor planning, joining in
LunchOpening packaging, noise, smells, eating enough
HomeworkGetting started, hand endurance, organizing materials
BathingWater, hair washing, teeth, nails, sequencing
BedtimeWinding down, sensory environment, falling asleep

This is the reframe worth carrying. An OT working on core strength isn't interested in core strength. They're interested in a child who can sit through a lesson without sliding off the chair. The exercise is never the goal — the participation is, and if a therapist can't tell you which part of the day a goal is aimed at, that's the question to ask.

What OT targets

Nine domains. Most children work on two or three at a time, and a good therapist is explicit about which and why.

Fine motor and hand skills

The building blocks of everything a child does with their hands, developed in a fairly predictable order.

  • Grasp development and refinement
  • In-hand manipulation — shifting and rotating an object within one hand
  • Bilateral coordination — two hands doing different jobs at once
  • Hand and finger strength, and endurance over time
  • Scissors, fasteners, utensils, tool use
  • Separation of the two sides of the hand

What you'd see at homeAvoiding coloring and building, tiring fast, an awkward or fisted pencil grip, difficulty with buttons and zips, dropping things, using two hands where one should do.

Handwriting and written output

Its own domain, because it draws on motor skill, visual perception, memory, and endurance simultaneously — and because it gates so much of school.

  • Letter formation, sizing, spacing, and line use
  • Pencil grip and pressure
  • Copying from board and from text
  • Speed and endurance across a full page
  • Keyboarding and speech-to-text as legitimate alternatives

What you'd see at homeHomework that takes three times as long as it should, complaints of hand pain, work far below what the child can say aloud, refusal to write at all.

Postural control and motor foundations

The stability underneath everything else. A body working hard to stay upright has less available for the task in front of it.

  • Core and trunk stability; shoulder girdle stability for hand use
  • Balance and postural reactions
  • Motor planning and praxis — knowing how to organize a new movement
  • Crossing midline; body awareness in space
  • Endurance for sitting, standing, and carrying

What you'd see at homeSlumping, leaning on furniture and people, W-sitting, fatiguing quickly, clumsiness, avoiding playgrounds, difficulty learning any new physical routine.

Sensory processing and regulation

How a nervous system takes in and responds to information, and what a child needs to stay in a state where learning is possible.

  • Over-responsiveness — sound, touch, texture, light, smell, movement
  • Under-responsiveness — not registering pain, temperature, mess, or being called
  • Sensory seeking — crashing, spinning, chewing, constant movement
  • Discrimination — telling apart what is being felt or heard
  • Interoception — noticing hunger, thirst, needing the bathroom, and rising emotion
  • Recovery after dysregulation, and prevention before it

What you'd see at homeClothing battles, haircuts and nail cutting as crises, meltdown after a busy environment rather than during it, never seeming to notice injury, unable to sit still.

Self-care and daily living

The domain most directly tied to independence and to dignity, and the one most worth prioritizing over the long run.

  • Dressing, fasteners, shoes, coats
  • Toileting — the motor, sensory, and sequencing pieces
  • Bathing, hair washing, teeth, nail care
  • Self-feeding and mealtime participation
  • For teens: cooking, laundry, money, transport, medication routines

What you'd see at homeNeeding full help long past the expected age, resistance to grooming tasks, morning routines that consume the whole household.

Feeding and mealtimes

Often shared with a speech-language pathologist. OT tends to lead on the sensory, motor, and positioning side.

  • Seating and positioning — feet supported, trunk stable
  • Oral motor skill for chewing and managing texture
  • Sensory tolerance of new foods, smells, and mess
  • Utensil use and self-feeding
  • Mealtime structure and reducing pressure

What you'd see at homeA shrinking food list, gagging at textures, refusing to sit, needing to be fed long past expectation, meals that end in tears.

Visual motor and visual perception

What the brain does with visual information, and how it drives the hands. Distinct from whether the eyes see clearly.

  • Visual motor integration — copying shapes, letters, and designs
  • Figure-ground — finding a thing among other things
  • Form constancy, spatial relations, visual memory
  • Tracking and shifting gaze between board and paper
  • Adapting materials for reduced clutter and higher contrast

What you'd see at homeLosing place when reading or copying, difficulty finding things in a drawer, letter reversals persisting, puzzles and mazes avoided.

Executive function and organization

Getting started, keeping going, and switching when the plan changes — approached through daily routines rather than as an abstract skill.

  • Task initiation and breaking a task into a first step
  • Sequencing multi-step routines
  • Organizing materials and space
  • Transitions and time awareness
  • Self-monitoring and self-evaluation

What you'd see at homeStanding in a room unable to begin, losing everything, morning and bedtime routines needing step-by-step prompting every single day.

Play, participation, and environment

Play is a child's primary occupation, and the environment is often the fastest thing to change.

  • Play skills, imagination, and playing alongside and with others
  • Access to playgrounds, sports, and community activities
  • Adaptive equipment, seating, and assistive technology
  • Home and classroom modification
  • Sleep environment and routine

What you'd see at homePlaying alone or not at all, refusing birthday parties and playgrounds, a bedroom that never quite works, a classroom seat that isn't right.

How an evaluation works

Usually one to three sessions, plus a written report. The best part of it is the conversation about what your family actually wants to be different.

What happens

  • An occupational profile. A structured interview about the day — what works, what doesn't, what you've tried, and what you'd most want changed. This is where the goals come from, and a therapist who skips it is choosing goals for you.
  • Standardized assessment where it's useful. Common tools measure motor skills, visual motor integration, sensory processing patterns via caregiver and teacher questionnaires, and functional independence.
  • Clinical observation — watching your child move, play, write, and handle materials, including in the setting where the difficulty happens whenever that's possible.
  • Goal setting with you, ideally using a measure that scores progress against goals you named rather than against a norm table.
  • A written report with baseline, goals, recommended frequency, and discharge criteria.

The question that shapes the whole plan

When an evaluator asks what you'd most like to change, answer with a moment from your day rather than a skill. Not "his fine motor skills." Instead: "getting dressed takes forty minutes and we're late every morning," or "he can't open anything in his lunchbox so he doesn't eat."

Concrete moments produce concrete goals, and concrete goals are the ones you can tell have been met.

Approaches, sorted by how well they're supported

OT covers a lot of ground, and the evidence behind different approaches genuinely varies. This isn't a reason to distrust the field — it's the information that lets you ask better questions about a specific plan.

Well supported

Task-specific and occupation-based practice — practicing the actual activity, in the actual place. Consistently outperforms practicing underlying components in isolation.

Goal-directed training with family-chosen goals and measured progress.

Cognitive approaches such as CO-OP, where a child learns a strategy for solving motor problems themselves — strong evidence for coordination difficulty.

Constraint-induced and bimanual therapy for one-sided weakness in cerebral palsy.

Parent coaching in natural routines — as effective as or better than clinic-only models, and far more sustainable.

Environmental modification and assistive technology — the fastest-acting intervention in the whole field, and the most under-used.

Explicit handwriting instruction with self-evaluation, where handwriting is the goal.

Mixed or emerging

Ayres Sensory Integration, delivered in its manualized form by a trained therapist: some randomized trial support for individually chosen goals in autism, weaker support for broader developmental or academic claims. Ask whether the therapist is trained in the actual protocol, and what specific goal it's aimed at.

Interoception curricula — promising and increasingly used, with a young evidence base.

Regulation curricula such as zones and alert-style programs — widely used, sensible, still thin on trial evidence.

Sensory strategies in the classroom — the individual accommodations are well accepted even where the packaged programs are not.

Little support — ask hard questions

Weighted vests for attention or behavior. Repeatedly studied, repeatedly unimpressive.

Prescribed "sensory diets" applied as a fixed schedule regardless of response, and brushing protocols.

Therapeutic listening and auditory integration programs.

Primitive reflex integration as a treatment for learning or attention difficulties.

None of this means a therapist using them is acting in bad faith — many are widely taught. It means you should ask what outcome is expected, how it will be measured, and by when.

The single most useful question to ask

Whatever the approach: which part of our day will look different if this works, and when should I expect to see it?

Any approach that can answer that is worth trying. Any approach that can't is worth pausing over, regardless of how well-established it is.

What OT works on at each stage

Birth to three

Foundations, in the family's routines

Feeding, sleep, tolerating handling and positioning, early motor milestones, play, and calming. Delivered mostly through parent coaching in the home, because at this age the parent is the intervention and the therapist is the coach.

Preschool

Participation and pre-academics

Self-care independence, sitting and attending in a group, tool use, pre-writing, playground access, and managing sensory demands in a busy room. This is the window where dressing and toileting independence is most efficiently built.

Elementary

Access to the school day

Handwriting and keyboarding, endurance at a desk, organization of materials, regulation strategies that work in a classroom, lunchbox and bathroom independence, and playground participation with peers.

Middle and high school

Independence and self-knowledge

Executive function for a multi-teacher schedule, assistive technology, self-advocacy about what they need, hygiene and grooming routines, and the beginnings of cooking, laundry, money, and transport.

Transition to adulthood

The life they're actually heading into

Independent living skills, work readiness and job-site assessment, driving evaluation where relevant, community access, and the environmental adaptations that make an adult life workable. Start this at fourteen, not at twenty.

School OT and private OT are not the same thing

A frequent source of confusion, and of families being told they're already getting something they aren't.

  • School-based OT exists to support access to education. That's the legal standard. If a difficulty doesn't affect educational participation, it's outside the mandate — however much it affects home life.
  • Private OT can address anything the family and therapist agree matters: sleep, self-care, feeding, community participation, family routines.
  • Service intensity differs sharply. School OT is often consultative or delivered in short group sessions. Ask whether the minutes on the IEP are direct or consult, and where they happen.
  • Many families use both, which is reasonable — but ask the two therapists to speak to each other so goals aren't duplicated or working against each other.
  • Home-based private OT is often the highest-yield option for self-care, sleep, and routine goals, because that's where those occupations actually happen.

Recognizing strong OT

What good looks like

  • Goals come from your day and are written so you can tell whether they've been met
  • The therapist explains the reasoning, not just the activity
  • Parent coaching is built in — you leave knowing what to do this week
  • Progress is measured and shown to you, not asserted
  • Work happens in real contexts where it can, not only in a therapy room
  • The plan changes when something isn't working
  • There is a discharge conversation, and it happens
  • They tell you honestly when a different discipline should lead

Worth asking about

  • The same activities every week for a year with no change in plan
  • No home program, or one you were handed once and never revisited
  • Goals you can't picture — "improve sensory processing," "increase core strength"
  • A sensory diet prescribed at intake and never reassessed
  • Progress described only as "he's doing great" with nothing measured
  • Claims about outcomes outside OT's scope — reading level, behavior, attention as a whole
  • Parents kept in the waiting room as a matter of policy
  • No discharge criteria, and no conversation about what "done" would look like

Questions worth asking a therapist

  • Which part of our day are we aiming at, and what will it look like if this works?
  • How will we measure it, and how often will you show me?
  • What should we be doing at home — and what should we stop doing?
  • Should we change how we're talking to them at home — fewer instructions and questions, more observations? (See declarative language on the behavior page.)
  • What's your reasoning behind this particular approach?
  • How do you adapt when a child refuses or shuts down?
  • What would make you say we're finished here?
  • How do you coordinate with the school, and with our other therapists?
  • What's your experience with children who have this profile?

Practical matters

For home practice between sessions, the free practice mirror plays a clip your therapist filmed see-through over the live camera.

Frequency and duration

Weekly is common; some goals do better with short intensive bursts and some with less frequent visits and a strong home program. What matters more than frequency is what happens between sessions — distributed practice in real routines consistently outperforms a single clinic hour, which is why parent coaching is a feature rather than a shortcut.

Finding an OT

  • Look for a licensed or registered occupational therapist, and ask about pediatric experience specifically.
  • Ask about additional training relevant to your child — feeding, sensory integration certification, assistive technology, hand therapy, or driving assessment for older teens.
  • Ask where they work: clinic, home, school, or a mix. Match the setting to the goal.
  • Ask what happens if your child can't tolerate the clinic environment. A good answer involves adapting; a poor one involves your child needing to be ready first.

Paying for it

  • Evaluations and treatment are billed under specific codes — verify coverage by code and place of service before you start, as described on the therapy funding page.
  • Ask whether evaluations count against annual visit limits. They often do.
  • A letter of medical necessity tied to a functional problem is far more likely to be paid than a general request for OT.
  • School OT costs nothing and is written into the IEP. Private OT can run alongside it.

Before you close this

If this page felt like a lot, here is the whole of it in one line: OT is the discipline that asks what your day is actually like, and then changes something so it goes better.

You already know which moments are hardest — the forty-minute morning, the lunchbox that never gets opened, the homework that ends in tears. That is the evaluation, done. Bring those three moments to a first appointment and a good therapist can build an entire plan from them.

This is a map, not medical advice. Whether occupational therapy is appropriate for a particular child, and what should be worked on first, is a decision for clinicians who have assessed them. Scope of practice, licensure, and school service rules vary by state.

Where evidence for a specific approach is limited, this page says so. That isn't a reason to avoid it — it's a reason to ask what outcome is expected, how it will be measured, and by when.
