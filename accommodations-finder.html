The accommodations finder:
start with the difficulty, not the diagnosis

Most accommodation lists are organized by label, which is no help when you're sitting in a meeting knowing what's wrong but not what to call it. This one starts where you start, with what you actually see, and gives you language you can ask for word for word.

Two things before you use it. Ask for a small number of accommodations that will actually be delivered rather than a long list that won't. And say the phrase out loud in the meeting — specific wording is what gets written into the document, and what's written is what's enforceable.

Reading this as a teacher or therapist

Everything here can be proposed by anyone at the table. What differs is weight: a written parent request starts formal timelines and creates an obligation to respond, while a professional's suggestion is advice the team can take or leave.

Which makes the fastest route counterintuitive — handing a family the exact phrase, so they can ask for it in writing, often achieves more than raising it yourself.

One last thing

You already know what is hard for your child. That was always the difficult part, and you have had it all along.

This page only exists to hand you the words, and words can be borrowed. Take three, ask for them clearly, and see what happens.

This is a map, not legal advice. Whether a given support belongs in an IEP, a 504 plan, or a health plan depends on your child and your state. Your state's parent training and information center gives free local guidance.

Adapt every phrase to your own child. A specific accommodation that names your child's actual situation always beats a generic one copied from a list.
