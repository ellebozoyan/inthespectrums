What we mean by spectrums

The name is plural, and the preposition is deliberate.

On the spectrum is a phrase a great many people use with real warmth, and it has helped families find each other. It does, though, describe a line. One dimension, with people placed somewhere along it, and over time it has narrowed to mean one condition, and can land as a measurement.

We wanted a name that opened outward instead. One with room enough for everybody, including the people who have never once thought of themselves as being on any spectrum at all.

A life is far more than a single spectrum. There are dozens of them, and every one of us is somewhere in all of them at once — shifting, most days, more than we notice.

SensingHow sound, light, texture and smell arrive, and how much of it you can take.
MovingHow your body goes where you send it, what that costs you, and how much moves inside even when very little moves outside.
Breathing and sleepingHow well the two jobs that underwrite everything else are going.
EatingWhat your body will accept, and what it does with it.
InteroceptionHow clearly you know what is happening inside you.
CommunicatingHow much of what you mean makes it out, and arrives intact.
FeelingHow fast it comes, how big it gets, how long it stays.
AttentionWhat pulls it, what holds it, and whether you can aim it on purpose.
JoyWhat brings it, how much you can hold, and what it takes to reach.
DreamingWhat you long for, what lights you up, and what feels possible — today, and further out.
DoingTurning some of that into a real thing: a skill, a job, a friendship, a Saturday you chose.
BelongingBeing wanted somewhere, by someone, for who you actually are, and wanting to be there, seeing and accepting them fully back.

And more than these.

Nobody sits in the middle of all of them. Not one person who has ever lived. You are unusual on several dimensions, and you have already built your days around them. The seat you always choose. The noise you avoid. The way you need instructions written down. Ordinary adjustments, made without calling them adjustments.

Which means every one of us is somewhere unusual. Difference is not the exception here. It is the material we are all made of.

That is what in the spectrums means. Not placed on a line and measured. Living inside a space with many dimensions, moving around it all day, as everyone does.

We are not points on a line. We are surfing the waves of a life — carrying real burdens, holding real gifts, and moving all the time, inside and out.

And the spectrums do not stay still or stay separate. They bend and tangle as we move among family, school, work and community. A person is different at home than at school. A day that would have been fine on Tuesday is not fine on Thursday. Two people in a room might change what each of them can do.

None of that is instability, and none of it is anyone failing. It is what being alive among other people looks like, and it means the conditions around a person are always at least partly changeable, which is where nearly all of our influence lives.

This is not a way of saying we are all the same

Being in the spectrums is universal. What differs — enormously, and consequentially — is what a particular position needs, has recognized, asks for, and actually gets. Some people need a little, occasionally, and can arrange it themselves. Some need much, constantly, from many people, for their whole lives.

Those are genuinely different situations, and naming the difference honestly is not a judgment. It is how you work out what is actually needed, and go and find the care, the supports and the people who can provide it.

What they share is the shape of the thing. Somebody uses a wheelchair. Somebody else uses a chair, and a familiar route to work, and a friend who knows to text before calling. Everyone is being held up by something. We only call some of it equipment.

Which means nothing on this site belongs to a separate world. It is more of what everyone already leans on, or a different shape of it, built to reach further, so that it gets all the way to the person it is for.

And the gap is not fixed

The distance between what a person needs and what the world offers has closed a great deal, and most of it closed within living memory. Not steadily, not everywhere, and in some places it has widened again. Where it closed, people closed it. Curb cuts, captions, communication devices, inclusive classrooms, and the plain expectation that a person belongs in the room all had to be argued for, usually by families and by people with disabilities themselves, usually against resistance.

That work is unfinished, and some of what remains is genuinely hard. But it is something people build, not something that simply happens to us, which means it moves when we move it. And a lot of what you do for one person quietly widens the door for everyone who comes after them.

You are not late. Whatever has already happened, the next thing you do still counts — and it counts more than you can see from here.

Who this is actually for

Worth saying plainly, because people arrive assuming they are in the wrong place.

Nobody has to qualify.

If your child has a lisp and nothing else, this is for you. If they cannot hop, or will not walk on grass, or eat six things and no others, or need their hands over their ears in a supermarket, for you. If something is harder than it looks like it should be and you have no idea whether it matters, that is exactly the situation this was written for.

You do not need a diagnosis. You do not need a referral, a label, a waiting list, or a professional's permission to want better information about your own child. A great many people spend a year wondering whether their concern is big enough to count. It is.

And if your family is carrying layer upon layer. This was built for you especially.

Several diagnoses. Several therapies. A calendar nobody can hold in their head. A child whose needs cross medicine, education, communication, feeding, sleep, safety and behavior all at once, where every part affects every other part and no single professional sees the whole thing.

Not because your situation matters more. Because you are the least well served by everything else that exists.

General parenting advice does not go nearly deep enough. Condition-specific resources cover one layer beautifully and leave the other seven untouched. Almost nothing is written for the family managing all of it at once, on no sleep, while also being the only person who has read every report.

Both kinds of reader can use the same page. Somebody with one small question takes the paragraph that applies and closes the tab. A family with fifteen overlapping things takes the whole page, and the tools, and the templates, and comes back in six months when something else surfaces.

One difficulty is not too small to belong here. Fifteen is not too many.

What we are actually doing here

For in the spectrums, the aim is not to arrive anywhere. It is to keep going, and to get better at it.

  • Becoming more aware of ourselves. What we need, what is too much, what helps, and how to say so.
  • Connecting with other people, and contributing something to them. Not being managed by them. Being with them.
  • Being nurtured, and being challenged. Both. Either one alone stops working quickly.
  • Growing, and failing. Failing is not the interruption of the process. It is most of the process, for every single one of us. The people who get somewhere are simply the ones who were allowed to keep going afterward.
  • Having compassion while we get back up, for the person, and for ourselves, which is usually the harder one.
  • Finding the one thing we learned, naming it out loud, and beginning again.

That last sequence is the whole method, and it is the same whether you are three or forty-three. It is what the Maplewood books are about. It is what the strategies on this site are for. And it applies to the adults at least as much as to the children, because a family learning this together does much better than a family where only the child is expected to.

What progress actually looks like

The honest version, because the hopeful version has been unhelpful to a great many families.

We know this is a lot. We know you are tired, and that you have already done more than anyone outside your house will ever see. That is exactly why this exists, so that some of it is already worked out by the time you get here, and you can spend what you have left on your person instead of on the search.

The nearest thing to it is a Rubik's Cube. You will do much and see nothing. Then something will go backward. Then a small thing will change, and you will not be sure whether it counts, and you will not be able to say which of the eleven things you are doing caused it.

Then more nothing. Then backward again. On a cube that is not a setback, it is the method: you routinely have to break up a finished side to get anywhere at all.

And then, some ordinary Tuesday, you will look up and notice that a whole side of it has come together — a thing that was impossible last year has quietly become just another part of the day, and you will not be able to point to the turn that did it.

That is not a failure of your attention. That is what this kind of change looks like from the inside.

Which is the argument for continuing on the days you can see no reason to. Not because effort guarantees an outcome. It does not, and anyone promising otherwise is selling something. But because the turning is the part that belongs to you, and this kind of picture almost never comes together while you are standing over it, watching.

Two things follow from this that matter more than they sound.

Early is worth more than perfect. Putting supports in place before you can prove they are working is a bet made without evidence, and it is the bet with the best odds, because the windows are real, and because a support that turns out to be unnecessary costs far less than one that arrives four years late.

And you cannot do everything. Families are handed a list that no person could complete, and then feel they are failing at all of it simultaneously. A small number of things done consistently beats everything done once. Choosing what not to do this year is not giving up. It is the only way anything gets done properly.

What is actually within your control

Less than you were promised, and considerably more than it feels like at three in the morning.

You cannot make a body develop faster, undo a diagnosis, or make a system move at the speed it should. You can decide what gets asked for and in what order. You can put things in writing. You can change how the adults around a person speak to them. You can keep a record. You can get on a list four years early. You can find the person who will actually help, and stop spending time on the one who will not. You can notice the early signs, and act in the window where acting still works.

None of that is small. Most of it is free. And nearly all of the difference between a good outcome and a poor one, in the parts anyone can influence, sits inside that list.

You will not do all of it. Nobody does all of it. Do the next reachable thing, and let that be enough for today, because done steadily, it turns out to be a lot.

Why any of this matters

A life is a gift, and it is happening now, not once someone is easier to include, not once the hard parts are solved. Every person's life is already the real one, already underway, already worth being fully inside.

So everyone should get to be in it. Safely. Understood by the people nearby. Able to refuse, able to say what happened, able to be known. Holding whatever tools make those things possible, without having to earn them first.

That is the whole of what this is for. The rest of the site is just the detail.
