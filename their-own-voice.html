Their own voice:
talking with your child about their own life

Every other page on this site is adults talking about children. This one runs the other direction — how families talk with a child about their own brain and body, and how ownership of that story gets handed over across childhood.

Before you read any of this

There is no single right way to do this, and this page isn't going to pretend otherwise. What you tell your child, when, and in what words is one of the most personal decisions a family makes. It depends on your child, your values, your culture, your faith, what your family has already been through, and what your child is ready for on a given Tuesday.

So what follows is options with reasoning, not instructions. Where families differ, this page lays out several approaches and what each one tends to do well and cost. The area of actual agreement is much smaller than most resources imply, and it's marked as such.

Reading this as a therapist or teacher

Families ask for this language constantly and rarely get it. Having a few of these phrasings ready, and being clear that the framing is theirs to choose — is often the most useful thing a professional offers in a hard week.

The communication access section is written to be quotable in a report or a team meeting.

The small area most people agree on

This list is short on purpose. Nearly everything else is a judgment call that belongs to you.

  • Children notice things about themselves. They notice they go to rooms other children don't, that adults talk in low voices, that something makes grown-ups worried. Whatever you decide to say, you're not deciding whether they notice — only what fills the gap.
  • Something is better than silence. The most common regret reported by adults with disabilities isn't the words their parents used. It's that nobody said anything at all, so they built their own explanation out of whatever was available.
  • Tone carries as much as content. A solemn delivery communicates this is a tragedy no matter which words follow. A matter-of-fact one communicates this is manageable and we can talk about it.
  • It's a conversation, not an event. Almost no family finds one big talk works. Most of this happens in cars, at bedtime, in small pieces, over years, returned to as a child changes.
  • Their preference about language wins. Once a child can express a view about what they want to be called and how they want to be described, that view outranks any style guide, including the one this site uses.

One word, four very different children

Worth naming early, because it changes how much explanatory weight a diagnostic label can actually carry.

Picture four children in the same classroom, all carrying the same diagnosis on their paperwork. One doesn't use speech, and is the most socially outgoing person in the room. One talks in long, detailed paragraphs and finds ten minutes of conversation with a peer completely exhausting. One takes every instruction completely literally, and is baffled when a teacher says “in a minute.” One is unbothered by noise or crowds, and needs to know exactly what is happening next.

They share a word. They share very little else. Any explanation built around what that word means will fit at most one of them, and will quietly misdescribe the other three.

This matters practically. A diagnostic label is largely an administrative and medical key. It opens doors to services, funding, protections, and research, and it tells a clinician roughly where to start. It is not a description of a person, and on its own it gives a child very little they can actually use about themselves. Which is one honest reason some families don't build their explanation around the word at all, and instead describe the specific thing in front of them: what's easy, what's hard, what helps.

The same holds anywhere several children share a label — a classroom, a clinic waiting room, a support group. A child who hears the word explained in general terms and doesn't recognize themselves in it may conclude the explanation is wrong, or that they are.

A voice is not the same as speech

This page is about a child having their own voice. For a significant number of children, that voice will not be speech, or will not be speech alone, and how early that is recognized shapes almost everything else.

Some children learn to speak on schedule. Some speak years later than anyone predicted, including children who were told they never would. Some will speak in a quiet room with a familiar person and lose access to speech entirely when stressed, tired, or frightened. And some will never use speech as their primary way of communicating.

Nobody can reliably tell you at two or three which of those a particular child will be. Predictions made at that age turn out wrong often enough, in both directions, that most families and clinicians would rather act on what a child needs today than on a guess about where they will be at ten.

Which is the argument for giving access now

Augmentative and alternative communication — a speech-generating device, a symbol system, signs, a low-tech board — belongs alongside speech from the very beginning, for any child whose speech is delayed or uncertain.

The research here is unusually consistent, and it is good news: children who are given AAC tend to make gains in spoken language too. Giving a child a way to communicate supports speech. The two work together.

And starting now buys something immediate. A child with a full communication system can want something, object to something, share an idea, tell a joke, and say where it hurts — today, years before anyone knows how their speech will turn out. Every month of access is a month of being understood, and of being more deeply connected to their people.

This matters especially where autism is part of the picture. Differences in social communication and social interaction are one of the two pillars the diagnosis actually rests on — the other being restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior and interest. Which means a reliable way to communicate and connect is not a support sitting at the edges of autism. It is aimed straight at the center of it, and it is among the most impactful interventions a family can pursue.

So the working principle many families and clinicians land on is simple: give access to a full communication system now, and keep working on speech at the same time. Both, together, from the start.

What "access" actually means

  • Available from day one. A child gets a way to communicate because they are a person with things to say. Readiness tests, mastering a set of pictures first, and proving they understand cause and effect are all hurdles that can be set aside — access comes first, and understanding grows through using it.
  • A vocabulary big enough to say anything, not just to request things. A system limited to asking for snacks teaches a child that communication is a vending machine. Commenting, refusing, asking questions, joking, complaining, and telling someone they're wrong all need to be available.
  • Words for no, stop, and I don't like it — reachable in a couple of taps. A system that can only make requests cannot report that something is wrong.
  • Adults using it too. Modeling language on the device, all day, without demanding the child use it back. Nobody learns a language they've only ever been quizzed in.
  • Present everywhere. Charged, within reach, at lunch, on the playground, on the bus, in the bath if that's what it takes, and in a hospital bed.
  • Never removed as a consequence. Taking away a communication device as a punishment is taking away a person's voice.

And it is theirs, not the team's

A device voice is a voice. Children have views about which one they want. Many devices offer a choice, and a child who is going to speak with that voice for years should get a say in how it sounds. The same goes for what the device is called, what it looks like, what goes on the case, and who is allowed to touch it.

These sound like small things. They are the difference between a child who has a device and a child who has a voice.

Where families differ

Four decisions, each with real reasoning on more than one side. None of these has a consensus answer, and families change approach as children grow.

One · How you frame it

Everyone is different

"Everybody finds some things easy and some things hard. For you, noticing small details is easy and loud rooms are hard. For the person sitting next to you, it's completely the other way round. That's just how people are, and it's all okay."

Does well
Fits a child whose profile doesn't match the label's stereotype. Avoids defining a person by a category. Works when the children around them have needs that look nothing like theirs. Doesn't hand a child a word they may later reject or want to claim on their own terms.
Trades off
The specific word eventually becomes useful, for finding community, understanding themselves, and asking for what they're entitled to. Some adults have said they wished they'd had it sooner. Most families using this framing add the word later rather than never.

Name it early and plainly

"You have autism. That's the word for why sounds are so loud for you, and why you know everything there is to know about trains. Lots of people share that word, grown-ups too."

Does well
Gives a child language for their own experience and access to a community. Prevents finding out by accident later, which some adults describe as the harder version. Makes the word ordinary rather than loaded.
Trades off
A label carries other people's assumptions with it, and a young child can't control who they tell. It may not fit how your child experiences themselves, and it can become the whole explanation for everything they do.

Follow their questions

"That's a really good question. Do you want the long answer? There's a word for how your brain works, if you'd like to know it."

Does well
Meets a child exactly where their curiosity is, and lets them control the pace. Nothing arrives before it's wanted.
Trades off
Some children never ask, including children who've already sensed the topic is uncomfortable. Waiting for a question can become waiting indefinitely, so it helps to open small doors rather than only answering.

Two · Whether the word comes from you

Some families are clear they want to be the source, so the word arrives with warmth attached. Others are comfortable with it emerging from school, a clinician, or the child's own reading, and would rather not make an announcement out of it. Both happen, and both work.

The practical consideration most families weigh: information a child finds by accident — an overheard call, a report left open — tends to feel like something that was being kept from them, whatever was actually intended. If you're not planning to raise it directly, it's worth thinking about what your child is likely to encounter and when.

Three · How much detail, and when

There's a real spread here, from families who explain the full clinical picture as soon as a child can follow it, to families who stay entirely with lived experience — what's hard, what helps, and never make the medical detail central.

A useful test some families use: is this information my child can do something with? Knowing that headphones help is usable at seven. Knowing a genetic variant name usually isn't, until it becomes relevant to a decision they're making. Detail that a child can act on tends to land differently from detail they can only carry.

Four · Who else knows, and who decides

Whether to tell teachers, extended family, friends' parents, and the child's own friends is a genuine values question, and families weigh privacy, access to support, and their child's future control over their own information differently.

What shifts over time is who holds that decision. Most families move from deciding for a young child, to deciding with an older one, to the young person deciding entirely, and the age at which that transfer happens varies widely and reasonably.

Some ways families answer the hard questions

These arrive in the car and at bedtime. Several possible answers to each — they're different in approach rather than better and worse, and the one that fits your child is the right one.

"Why am I different?"

Universal framing — "Everyone's different from everyone. You and I are different, you and your best friend are different. Some things are easier for you than for other people and some are harder, and that's true of every single person I know."

Specific framing — "Because your brain is built in a way that has a name, and that name comes with some things that are harder and some that are brilliant."

Turn it back — "What made you think about that today?" Sometimes the question is about a specific thing that happened, and answering the big version misses what they actually needed.

useful to knowChildren generally know when something is different, so an answer that flatly denies it can teach them not to raise the topic again. Beyond that, the framing is yours.

"Am I stupid?"

Name the feeling first — "That sounds like a horrible way to feel. Tell me what happened." Then the correction, once they feel heard.

Separate the two things — "Being smart and finding reading hard are different things. You can be brilliant and still need audiobooks — those aren't the same question at all."

Name the effort — "What you're feeling is what it's like to work harder than the people around you and have nobody notice. That's exhausting, and it isn't the same as being stupid."

Worth knowingA fast reassurance can land as not being heard. Most families find some version of acknowledging the feeling first makes the reassurance stick better — though a child who just wants a quick no from you is also telling you something.

"Will I always be like this?"

Honest and steady — "You'll always have your brain. How much it gets in your way changes a lot, and mostly it gets easier as you learn what helps and get more say over your own life."

Focused on change — "You're going to change a huge amount, the way everybody does. Some of the hard bits get easier, some stay, and you get better at handling them."

useful to knowThe main thing families weigh here is what a promise costs later if it doesn't hold. Whatever you say, saying something you'll still stand behind in five years is the safer bet.

"Why do I have to go to therapy?"

Goal-framed — "Because there are things you want to be able to do that are harder for you than they should be, and that's what these people help with."

Ordinary-framed — "Lots of people work on things with someone who knows how. Some people have a tutor, some have a coach. This is yours."

Open to their view — "If it stops being useful, tell me and we'll talk about it properly."

Worth knowingIf you offer the third one, it works best when objections actually get taken seriously — otherwise children learn their view doesn't change anything, which is its own lesson.

"Why can't I do what my sibling does?"

Honest about unfairness — "Some things are harder for you and easier for them, and some are the other way round. It isn't fair, and I'm not going to pretend it is."

Focused on their own path — "You're on different roads. What is it you actually want to be able to do? Let's work out how you get there."

Concrete and specific — Sometimes the question is about one thing — riding a bike, going to a party, and can be answered as that one thing rather than as a statement about their whole life.

useful to knowChildren tend to notice forced equivalence, so this is one where many families find honesty lands more comfortably than balance.

"Do I have to tell people?"

Theirs to decide — "It's your information. Some people you'll want to tell, some you won't, and you get to choose each time."

Practical — "Let's work out what you'd say if you did decide to, so you've got the words ready either way."

Worth knowingWhatever you decide about disclosure while they're young, this becomes entirely their call as they get older, and it helps to have practiced the mechanics before it matters.

Growing self-advocacy

Not a sequence and not a set of milestones. These are directions to grow in, and children move through them at very different rates, out of order, and not all the way, which is fine.

Foundation

My preferences change what happens

The root of all of it, and available long before language.

  • Real choices with real consequences, honored once made
  • Refusal noticed and responded to in whatever form it comes — turning away, pushing off, going still
  • Stopping when they say stop, including in play

Building

I have words for what I need

  • "No," "stop," "help," "break", in speech, signs, or on a device
  • Adults naming their own needs out loud, so it's visibly normal
  • The child asking for their own accommodation sometimes, rather than it always being arranged silently

Growing

I know what works for me

  • Strategies named as they work: "the timer helped — that's useful to know about yourself"
  • Small real-world asks: the waiter, the librarian, the coach
  • Some understanding of their own profile, in whatever framing your family uses

Extending

I can explain myself to someone else

  • Practicing "I need X because Y" with you, then with a teacher
  • Seeing their own school plan, if and when that fits your family
  • A defined role in their own meetings — reading a strengths list is a common first one

Toward adulthood

It's mine

  • Requesting their own accommodations, with you reviewing rather than sending
  • Knowing what their paperwork says and where it lives
  • Answering for themselves at their own appointments
  • Deciding what to disclose, to whom, and when

For children who won't reach the later stages

Plenty of children won't run their own meetings or request their own accommodations, and this isn't a list of things to be achieved. But almost every child can be moved further along it than adults tend to assume, and the foundation is available to everyone.

Self-determination for a person with significant support needs looks like having preferences that reliably change what happens. That's achievable for nearly anyone, and everything else is built on it.

Learning from adults with disabilities

Worth seeking out, and worth reading critically — as you would any source.

What they offer

Adults who have lived this can describe things no clinician and no parent can: what a particular intervention felt like from inside, what the silence felt like, what they wish had been different.

For your child, meeting an adult who is like them and is living a life does something no explanation from you can do.

They disagree with each other

Disabled communities hold a wide range of views, sometimes sharply opposed — about therapy, about language, about what should have been done differently. There is no single community position to defer to.

Read widely enough to hear the range rather than one voice, and treat it as perspective to weigh rather than instruction to follow.

Some of it may sting

You will encounter strong criticism of choices you've made in good faith, sometimes about things you researched carefully.

Worth sitting with rather than dismissing, and also worth remembering that another person's experience of a different childhood isn't automatically a verdict on yours.

On language

Many adults with autism, and many Deaf people, prefer identity-first language for themselves; many others prefer person-first. Both are used in good faith and neither is a mistake.

Your child's own preference, once they have one, is the one that settles it for them.

Dignity in the ordinary moments

Practices many families adopt, with the reasoning behind them. Take what fits.

  • Not discussing them within earshot. Comprehension often runs well ahead of expression, so a child who can't respond may still be taking in every word about their difficulties. Many families find it easier to keep those conversations for later than to judge each time what's being understood.
  • Wondering aloud rather than questioning. A day made mostly of instructions and questions is exhausting to be on the receiving end of. Statements and observations leave room for a person to enter a conversation on their own terms. See the behavior page.
  • Speaking to them rather than about them. When someone addresses you instead of your child, redirecting — "you can ask him" — teaches both of them something.
  • One adult at a time when things are hard. Several people talking to a dysregulated child multiplies the input without adding help.
  • Asking before sharing their stories, including the good ones. "Can I tell Grandma about the swimming?" is small, and it establishes that their story belongs to them.
  • Explaining before touching — personal care, moving equipment, adjusting braces, even where there's no answer. It models what respectful handling feels like, which is part of how a child recognizes handling that isn't.
  • Age-respectful materials and topics, whatever level a child is working at.
  • Repairing out loud when you get it wrong. "I talked about you like you weren't there. That wasn't fair." Repair tends to teach more than getting it right first time.

Sharing their story: what families weigh

What you share about your child, where, and with whom is a genuine family decision, and there is no right answer to it. Some families post regularly, to friends and relatives who live far away, to colleagues, to other families in the same situation, and get real things back: community, practical advice, people who understand, and a record of a life being lived. Others share almost nothing. Both are considered positions held by thoughtful people.

It's also a decision that shifts. What feels right in the first frightened year may feel different in the fifth, and what a family decides at four is not binding at fourteen. What follows isn't a rule. It's the set of things good to have thought about, so the choice is a made one rather than a default.

Who is actually seeing it

A closed group of parents facing the same thing is a different setting from a public account, which is different again from a page your child's future classmates can find. Worth knowing which one you're in, and checking occasionally — privacy settings change, groups grow, and screenshots travel.

What kind of thing it is

Families often draw a line somewhere rather than sharing everything or nothing. A birthday photo sits differently from a video of a hard moment, which sits differently again from medical detail, toileting, or a description of behavior. Deciding in advance where your line is means not deciding it at 11pm after a difficult day.

Whose story is which part

Some of it is yours — your experience of parenting, what you've learned, what you found hard. Some of it is theirs: their body, their diagnosis, their worst moments, their medical history. The first is yours to tell. The second is being told on their behalf, and it's worth being conscious of which one a given post is.

How they might feel reading it later

The most useful single question, and the one most families come back to. Imagine them at fourteen, or twenty-five, or with a partner scrolling back through it. Would they be glad this exists? Proud of it? Fine with it? Or would they wish it weren't there? You can't know for certain, but asking changes what gets posted.

It usually can't be taken back

Posts can be deleted. Screenshots, archives, reshares, and search results are much harder to undo, and often can't be. Worth weighing anything you're unsure about as though it will be permanent, because in practice a good deal of it is.

Whether you can ask them

If they're old enough to have a view, asking costs nothing and teaches something worth teaching: that their story is theirs. Honor a no. And notice the age at which this stops being your call altogether, for most families that shift happens earlier than expected.

What you need, and how else it could be met

The need behind sharing is real and legitimate — isolation is one of the hardest parts of this, and being seen matters, fair to ask whether a closed group, a friend, a parent community, or a therapist could meet the same need with less permanence attached. Sometimes yes, sometimes not.

What good it does

The honest other side of the ledger. Families find each other this way. Someone newly diagnosed reads a post at 2am and feels less alone. Distant relatives stay part of a child's life. Representation of disabled children living ordinary lives is scarce, and families sharing it is part of why that's improving.

Adults who were posted about

A number of adults with disabilities have written about discovering their childhoods published online without their consent, and what that was like. Their accounts aren't unanimous and shouldn't be treated as a verdict, but they are the only firsthand evidence available about how this lands years later, and they're worth reading before deciding.

Revisit it

What you decided when they were three doesn't have to be what you do at ten. Take things down if your view changes. Ask them when they're old enough. Treat it as a live decision rather than a policy you set once.

Most families have no idea this is the default

It is not an occasional lapse by a careless person. In a great many schools and clinics it is simply how the day runs. Therapists hand over in the doorway. Teams discuss progress around a table with the student sitting at it. Adults compare notes on what someone cannot yet do while that person listens to every word.

Parents rarely witness it, because it happens when they are not there, and when they are there, everybody is on their best behavior. So the assumption that it does not happen is completely reasonable, and usually wrong.

What it sets up, in order

  1. A person listens to several adults list, out loud, the things they are not succeeding at.
  2. Minutes later, one of those same adults presents something new, unfamiliar and hard.
  3. It goes badly.

That is not a coincidence and it is not a behavior problem. It is an entirely predictable response to being demoralized and then tested. Nobody performs well immediately after hearing an inventory of their failures read aloud by the people whose opinion they care about most.

And then the hard moment gets written down — as data about the person, rather than about the ten minutes that preceded it. The adults created the conditions and the person is recorded as having the difficulty.

This is the exact opposite of setting somebody up for success, and it happens all day, in rooms full of people who would be horrified to hear it described this way.

Which is why it is worth raising with a team explicitly rather than assuming good intentions will cover it. Nobody is doing it unkindly. They are doing it because it is efficient, because the person has never objected in words, and because nobody has ever asked them not to. Asking is usually enough.

How the team talks about a student

This one is worth raising with a school directly, because it is the difference between a person who grows up knowing adults are on their side and a person who grows up as a topic.

  • The conversation about what is working, what is not, and what happens next belongs out of earshot. Genuinely out, not across the classroom, not in the hallway with the door open, not in the car with them in the back.
  • Assume they hear everything. Plenty of people have hearing much better than yours, and plenty of understand far more than they can say. Adults routinely mistake a lack of response for a lack of comprehension, and the person is left listening to themselves being discussed.
  • Watch the handover in the doorway. Arrival and pick-up are where this happens most — two adults summarising a person's morning while that person stands between them.
  • No diagnoses, incidents or difficulties named in front of them unless they are part of the conversation and know they are.
  • One adult speaking at a time, ideally at eye level, with the others giving space.

The practice that turns this around

Not talking about someone in front of them leaves a gap. Something better can go in it.

Ask their permission, then say something good. In front of them, to the next adult:

"Is it okay if I tell your OT one thing that went really well today?"

Then say it, properly, while they listen.

And it does not have to be an achievement in the ordinary sense. Some of the best things to hand over are not:

"He did an amazing job using his words to tell us his body wasn't ready for PT this week."

That sentence does several things at once. It tells the next adult something true and useful. It tells the person that self-advocacy is the thing worth reporting, not compliance. And it makes being talked about in front of you an experience of being valued rather than assessed.

Ask first, every time. Permission is what separates this from the thing we are trying to replace, and sometimes the answer will be no, which is also worth honoring visibly.

For teams: the professional service model matters here too. See choosing behavior support on consultation versus direct services, and who is actually in the room.

Before you close this

You will not get every one of these conversations right, and you do not have to. What a person remembers is the direction of travel. That the adults around them kept handing back a little more, kept asking rather than assuming, and treated their view as worth having.

That is the whole aim: a person who eventually knows their own mind, can say what they need, and holds their own story. Every small handover moves that closer.

This is a set of options, not a method. Your child, your family, your words, your values. If something here doesn't fit, that isn't a mistake on your part. It means the page is describing a family that isn't yours.

The aim shared across every approach here is a person who eventually knows their own mind, can say what they need, and holds their own story.
