What to do first,
starting the day you're worried

You don't need a diagnosis, a referral, or certainty to begin. You need to start the long waits today, all at once, because the order you do these things in changes your child's timeline by years.

Why the sequence matters

These five clocks can all start on the same afternoon. Most families start them one at a time, because each step seems to depend on the last.

Typical wait, from request to answer

Early Intervention evaluation (under age 3)
45 days, by law
School district evaluation (age 3+)
30–60 school days
Audiology and vision
2–8 weeks
Developmental-behavioral pediatrics
6–12 months
Neuropsychological evaluation
6–18 months

Run in parallel, you have most of your answers inside a year. Run one at a time — waiting for the pediatrician before calling EI, waiting for EI results before joining a neuropsych list — the same five steps take close to three years, and they take them out of the window where intervention does the most good.

Don't wait for the sequence if any of this is happening

Call your pediatrician this week and say the word urgent. If you can't get through, an urgent care or emergency department is a reasonable next step for the first four.

  • Loss of skills your child already had — words, gestures, walking, feeding themselves — at any age
  • Staring spells, unresponsive episodes, stiffening, or rhythmic jerking
  • Sudden dramatic personality change, new obsessions, or abrupt refusal to eat, especially after an illness
  • Not responding to sound, or no reaction to a loud noise
  • Weight loss, or weight and growth flattening on the chart
  • Self-injury, or any behavior that puts your child or someone else in danger
  • You are afraid to leave your child alone, and that's new
  • You're not coping, or you're having thoughts that frighten you — this counts, and it goes on this list

Regression is the one that gets waited on and shouldn't be. "Let's watch it for a few months" is not the right answer to a skill that disappeared.

Six sentences that change what happens next

Systems respond to specific language. These are the phrasings that start clocks, create records, and make a no expensive enough that it usually becomes a yes. Say them plainly and put them in writing.

To the school district — starts the legal timeline

I am requesting a full initial evaluation of my child in all areas of suspected disability, under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act. My areas of concern are: [list them]. Please send me the consent forms and a copy of my parental rights. I understand the evaluation timeline begins when you receive my written consent, and I would like to sign it as soon as possible.

To the pediatrician — creates the chart record everything else rests on

I'd like my concerns documented in my child's chart today. Specifically: [what you're seeing, with dates]. I'd also like a developmental screening, a referral for a hearing evaluation, and referrals to developmental-behavioral pediatrics and neuropsychology. I understand the waits are long, which is why I'd like the referrals now rather than after we watch this for a few months.

To every waitlist — the call that moves you up it

Please add us to your cancellation list, and I want to be clear that we can come with under twenty-four hours' notice. Can you tell me what the current wait is, whether anything about our situation would move us up, and when I should check back? I'd also like the intake paperwork now so it's finished and on file the moment a slot opens.

When you're told to wait and see

I hear that this could resolve on its own. I'd like to proceed with the evaluation anyway, and I'd like today's recommendation to wait documented in the chart along with my request. If we're watching, I want to know exactly what we're watching for and the date we reassess.

After any phone call that mattered

Thank you for speaking with me today. I want to confirm my understanding of what we discussed: [summary of what was said and agreed, with the date]. Please let me know if I've captured anything incorrectly.

When you're told no

I understand this is being declined. Could you send me that decision in writing, including the reasons and the information it was based on? I'd also like a copy of my rights and the process for disagreeing with this decision.

Things you'll be told that you can decline

None of these are given in bad faith. They're what an overloaded system says to a family that hasn't yet learned it can say something back.

"Let's give it six months."

You can request the evaluation now. Development doesn't pause while you wait, and a normal evaluation result costs you nothing but an afternoon.

"Boys talk later."

Sometimes true, and not a reason to skip a hearing test. This one has cost a great many children two years.

"We need to try classroom interventions first."

Under federal law, a tiered support process cannot be used to delay or deny an evaluation once a parent requests one in writing.

"He's doing fine here."

A child who holds it together at school and falls apart at home is not fine — they're rationing. Say exactly that, and describe the evenings.

"You need a diagnosis before we can start services."

Early Intervention and school services are based on demonstrated need, not on a diagnostic label. Start now; name it later.

"There's a waitlist, we'll call you."

Ask for the cancellation list, ask what moves families up it, and put a recurring reminder in your phone to call back monthly.

"That won't be covered."

Ask for the denial in writing with the specific policy language. A large share of denials are overturned on appeal, and many are simply coding errors.

"You don't qualify — your income is too high."

True for some programs, not for others. Several states have a Medicaid pathway based on the child's disability that disregards parent income entirely. Ask by name.

Before you close this

You have already done the hardest part of this page. Noticing that something needed attention, and then going looking for what to do about it, is the step children wait longest for — and you took it today.

Everything after this is phone calls, forms, and follow-up. That is tedious rather than difficult, and it can be learned. Make one call this week. That's a real move, and it counts.

This is a map, not legal or medical advice. Timelines, program names, and eligibility rules differ by state and change over time — verify the specifics where you live. Everything here assumes the United States.

The checkboxes on this page are for working through in one sitting. They don't save when you close the page — print it if you want to keep it.

One last thing, and it belongs on this page: talk about your child, not over them. They hear more than any evaluation will capture.
