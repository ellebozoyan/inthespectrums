Programs and entitlements:
what exists, and when to ask for it

Some of what your family is entitled to arrives automatically. Most of it doesn't. This page covers the programs that exist in every state, the ones that turn on at particular ages, and — using Massachusetts as a worked example — how to find your own state's version of each.

The framework, in every state

These exist nationally under different names. Learning the category is what lets you find the local version.

Early Intervention

Birth to 3 · Federal entitlement

Evaluation and services for infants and toddlers with delays or diagnosed conditions, delivered mostly in the home. Parents can self-refer without a doctor. Evaluation and the resulting service plan must be completed within 45 days of referral.

Ask forAn evaluation, today, by phone. No diagnosis, no referral, no income test for the evaluation itself.

Special education and 504 plans

3 to 21 or 22 · Federal entitlement

An IEP provides specialized instruction and related services under IDEA. A 504 plan provides accommodations under civil rights law for a child who needs access but not specialized instruction. Both are enforceable; only the IEP includes services measured in minutes.

Ask forA full initial evaluation in all areas of suspected disability, in writing, by email.

Medicaid disability pathways

Any age · Varies by state · Often income-blind

Several routes let a child qualify for Medicaid based on their own disability rather than family income — commonly a Katie Beckett or TEFRA pathway, a home and community based services waiver, or a state buy-in program. Medicaid then acts as secondary insurance behind a commercial plan, absorbing copays, deductibles, and services the private plan excludes.

Ask forThe disability-based pathway by name. The single most common reason families miss this is assuming they earn too much.

Your state developmental disability agency

Childhood through adulthood · Eligibility-based, not an entitlement

Every state has an agency providing supports for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities: family support, respite, in-home skill building, day and employment programs, residential options. Services depend on eligibility criteria and, critically, on annual funding — being eligible is not the same as being funded.

Ask forAn eligibility determination as early as your state allows, and the waitlist policy in writing.

Supplemental Security Income and ABLE accounts

Any age · Income and asset rules apply

SSI is a monthly cash benefit for children with significant disability in low-income households, and it often carries Medicaid with it. At eighteen, eligibility is reassessed on the young person's own income rather than the family's, which is why many are denied as children and approved as adults. ABLE accounts let a person with a disability save without losing benefits.

Ask forA reassessment at eighteen even if you were denied before. This one changes for a great many families.

Vocational rehabilitation

Roughly 14 and up · Federal-state program

State vocational rehabilitation agencies fund job training, coaching, assistive technology, and sometimes college support. Pre-employment transition services are available to students with disabilities while still in high school, and this is routinely started far too late.

Ask forPre-employment transition services during high school, not after graduation.

Free help understanding all of it

Every state · No cost, no eligibility test

Every state has a federally funded Parent Training and Information Center for special education, and a Family-to-Family Health Information Center for the health system. There are also state deafblind projects, commissions for the blind and for the deaf, and disability-specific support centers. All free, all under-used.

Ask forNothing in particular — just call. This is the cheapest hour you'll spend on any of this.

Massachusetts, as a worked example

Your state has equivalents of most of these under different names. Reading one state's version in detail is the fastest way to know what to go looking for in your own.

MassHealth CommonHealth

Children and adults with disabilities · No income ceiling

The one most families have never heard of. CommonHealth provides MassHealth benefits to children and adults with disabilities who don't qualify for MassHealth Standard, and it has no income limit at all — families above 150% of the federal poverty level pay a sliding-scale monthly premium instead of being excluded. It can sit behind private insurance and cover copays, coinsurance, deductibles, and services the commercial plan won't.

Qualifying requires a disability determination. For a child, that means submitting the MassHealth Child Disability Supplement along with medical records, evaluations, and the current IEP to Disability Evaluation Services. Determinations can take up to ninety days.

Worth knowingGather the IEP and evaluations before you start; the strength of the disability documentation is what drives the determination.

DESE/DDS Residential Prevention Program

School-age students who are DDS-eligible

A collaboration between the state education and developmental services departments providing in-home and community-based therapeutic supports — wraparound services aimed at building skills, independence, and social integration across home, school, and community. It also supports families whose child is returning home from a residential school placement.

Eligibility requires DDS Children's Services eligibility and a demonstrated need for services to prevent a more restrictive out-of-home residential placement. Families participate actively, provide evaluations and the current IEP, and share what other services are already in place. Participants typically receive several hours a week of behavioral and skills-based support.

Worth knowingFunding depends on an annual legislative appropriation, and the program runs an interest list with periodic open interest periods rather than continuous enrollment. Get on the list when it opens, even if you're unsure.

Children's Autism Medicaid Waiver

Young children with autism · Medicaid-eligible

Run by the DDS Autism Division for children with an autism spectrum disorder from birth through the early elementary years who are Medicaid eligible and at risk of institutionalization. It funds intensive in-home services and supports, including applied behavior analysis and Floortime, for up to three years.

Worth knowingFamilies must apply — this is not offered automatically, and the age window is narrow enough that late awareness means missing it entirely.

DDS eligibility, and the autism expansion

Children from age 5 · Adults from 22

DDS serves children aged five to twenty-two who have an intellectual or closely related developmental disability producing substantial functional limitations. For adults, a 2014 change expanded eligibility beyond intellectual disability to include developmental disabilities such as autism, Prader-Willi syndrome, and Smith-Magenis syndrome. Adult autism eligibility requires a severe chronic disability arising before age twenty-two with substantial functional impairment in three or more major life activity areas.

Worth knowingState guidance asks districts to encourage families to apply for adult DDS eligibility by age seventeen and a half. Unlike special education, adult DDS supports are not an entitlement — they depend on available funding, which is exactly why the lead time matters.

DDS Regional Autism Support Centers

Any family · No eligibility requirement

Regional centers funded by DDS offering training, workshops, activities, and family connection for children and adults with autism across the state. Free, local, and useful long before any formal eligibility is established.

Worth knowingYou don't need DDS eligibility to use a support center. Start here while other applications are pending.

Chapter 688 — the Turning 22 law

Two years before special education ends

Massachusetts law requiring a transition planning process to begin at least two years before a student graduates or turns twenty-two, whichever comes first. The school district makes a referral to the Bureau of Transition Planning and to adult agencies, which may include DDS, the Massachusetts Rehabilitation Commission, or the Department of Mental Health.

Worth knowingThe referral does not equal an application. Families still have to apply separately to whichever adult agency they're being referred to, and services remain subject to appropriation. The referral opens the conversation; it does not secure anything.

Where Massachusetts families start

The Federation for Children with Special Needs is the state's parent training and information center — free help with special education, health care navigation, and transition. Local Arc chapters, the regional autism support centers, and your DDS area office are the other three phone numbers worth having.

The ages when something changes

Almost every one of these has a lead time of a year or more. The dates that matter are when you start, not when the birthday arrives.

Birth to 3

Early Intervention

Self-refer immediately. Begin the handoff to the school district at least six months before the third birthday — transition planning is required well before the day services end.

Age 3

School district takes over

Preschool special education begins. This is a different system with a different standard, and services frequently shrink at this handoff unless someone is watching.

Age 5

State disability agency eligibility opens in many states

Apply as soon as your state allows. Waitlists for waivers and family supports run years in some places, and the clock starts at application, not at need.

Age 14

Transition planning enters the IEP

Federal law requires transition goals by sixteen and many states start at fourteen. Contact vocational rehabilitation about pre-employment transition services now, while your child is still in school.

Age 17 to 17½

Adult agency applications

Apply for adult developmental disability services. In Massachusetts, state guidance points to seventeen and a half. Determinations and funding decisions take many months.

Before 18

Decision-making, benefits, and legal status

Understand supported decision-making, health care proxy, and limited guardianship before anyone files for full guardianship — it is not the only option and often not the right one. Reapply for SSI, which is reassessed on the young person's own income at eighteen. Open an ABLE account if it fits.

Two years before exit

Formal transition referral

The district refers to adult agencies. You still apply separately. Adult services are eligibility-based and funding-dependent, not an entitlement — this is the hardest cliff in the entire system and the one worth preparing for earliest.

Age 22

Special education ends

Whatever adult supports are in place on that day are what continues. Everything that wasn't arranged in advance now starts from the beginning.

The sentence worth internalizing

Special education is an entitlement — the district must provide it. Adult disability services generally are not; they are eligibility-based and depend on annual appropriations. That distinction is the single most important thing to understand about the years after twenty-two, and it's why applications go in years early rather than when the need becomes urgent.

Finding your own state's version

  • Call your state's parent training and information center first. Every state has one, it's free, and they know the local program names, the current waitlists, and which office actually answers.
  • Search the category, not the brand. "[Your state] Medicaid waiver children developmental disability," "[your state] Katie Beckett," "[your state] developmental disabilities agency eligibility."
  • Ask three families whose children are five years older than yours. They know which programs are worth the paperwork and which have a seven-year list.
  • Apply for things you're not sure you need. Waitlists are the currency here. Declining a spot later costs nothing; joining a list late costs years.
  • Write down every eligibility determination and denial. Denials get appealed, criteria change, and children become eligible later for programs that turned them down before.
  • Re-ask annually. Programs open, close, expand, and change criteria with each budget cycle. A no from three years ago is not a no now.
Before you close this

Most of this is applications, waiting, and asking again. It is tedious rather than hard, and tedious is something you can do in small pieces on ordinary days.

One phone call this week puts you further along than a perfect plan you never start. Programs open, criteria change, and people who keep asking get answers.

This is a map, not legal advice. Program names, age ranges, eligibility criteria, and funding all change — sometimes annually, and always by state. Verify every detail here against your state agency's current published information before relying on it. The Massachusetts details were accurate to state sources at the time of writing and should be confirmed directly.

Eligibility for a program is not the same as funding for it. Ask about waitlists every time.
