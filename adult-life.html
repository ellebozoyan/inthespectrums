After school ends:
legal, money, and where life happens

The day special education ends, an entitlement becomes an application. Nothing is owed anymore — everything has to be qualified for, applied for, and funded. This is the part families are least prepared for and the part that most determines how an adult life actually goes, and almost all of it has to be started years before it is needed.

This is not legal or financial advice

Estate planning, trusts, guardianship, and public benefits are governed by state law and by federal rules that change. Getting any of it wrong can disqualify someone from benefits for years. This page is written so you know what exists and what to ask about — not so you can do it yourself.

For anything involving money or legal status, use an attorney who specializes in special needs planning or elder law. A general estate attorney who does not do this work regularly can cause serious and expensive damage with an ordinary, well-drafted will.

The cliff

School services are an entitlement: owed by law, to everyone who qualifies, regardless of budget. Adult services are eligibility-based: you apply, you may qualify, and you may still wait years for funding. It is the same person on both sides of a birthday.

Until school ends
  • Owed by law, whatever the budget
  • One agency coordinates it
  • Free, and it comes to you
  • A written plan, legally enforceable
  • A dispute process that works
  • Someone else notices what's missing
then
After
  • Apply, qualify, and then wait for funding
  • Several agencies, none of them in charge
  • Means-tested, and you go to it
  • A service plan that depends on appropriation
  • Appeals that are slower and narrower
  • Nobody notices but you

Which is why the work starts at fourteen, not at twenty-one. Nearly everything here has a waiting list, an application window, or a rule about when it must be set up. The families who land well are not the ones with more money — they are the ones who started earlier.

What changes at eighteen

Legally, everything. Your child becomes an adult with full decision-making rights, whether or not that reflects their actual capacity — and whether or not anybody has planned for it.

  • You lose the automatic right to information. Doctors, therapists, and colleges are no longer obliged to speak to you. Even where you are still doing everything, the legal answer is no unless something is signed.
  • They can sign contracts, consent to treatment, and refuse it. Including refusing things you believe they need.
  • Benefits are recalculated. SSI is reassessed on the young adult's own income and resources rather than the household's, so many people who were denied as children qualify at eighteen. Apply at eighteen even if you were turned down before.
  • Special education continues until the state's exit age even though they are legally adults, but educational rights may transfer to them at eighteen unless something is in place.
  • Selective Service registration is required of most people assigned male at birth at eighteen, regardless of disability, and failing to register can affect later benefits and employment.

The three documents that solve most of it

Before assuming guardianship is necessary, know that a great deal of what families actually need can be handled by three signed forms, if the person can understand and agree to them:

A health care proxy naming who can make medical decisions if they can't. A HIPAA release letting providers talk to you. A durable power of attorney for financial and legal matters.

These take an hour, cost little, keep the person's rights intact, and can be changed by them at any time. Ask an attorney whether they're sufficient before anyone files anything more restrictive.

Decision-making, from least to most restrictive

There is a legal principle behind this order: use the least restrictive option that actually works. Courts increasingly expect it, and starting at the bottom of this list is difficult to undo.

Least restrictiveSupported decision-making

The person keeps all their rights and chooses trusted people to help them understand options and communicate choices. Formal agreements are now recognized in many states. This is the default to start from, not the fallback.

Rights intactHealth care proxy and HIPAA release

Names who decides medically if they cannot, and lets providers speak with you. Revocable, cheap, and solves the single most common practical problem.

Rights intactDurable power of attorney

Authority over financial and legal matters, granted by the person. It can be broad or narrow and it can be withdrawn.

NarrowRepresentative payee

Someone appointed to receive and manage Social Security payments only. Does not affect any other right and is administered by the agency, not a court.

PartialLimited guardianship or conservatorship

A court removes decision-making in specific named areas only — medical, or financial — and leaves the rest. Where a court process is genuinely needed, ask for the narrowest version that addresses the actual risk.

Most restrictiveFull guardianship

A court finds the person legally incapacitated and transfers decision-making to someone else. They may lose the right to vote, marry, consent to or refuse treatment, sign anything, choose where to live, and manage their own money.

What to weigh before filing

Guardianship is sometimes genuinely necessary, and families who pursue it are usually acting out of well-founded fear about safety and exploitation. It is not a moral failing to seek it.

But it is worth knowing what it costs. It is a removal of civil rights. It is hard, slow, and expensive to reverse. It can make a person more vulnerable to a bad guardian rather than less vulnerable overall. And many adults who were placed under guardianship have described it as the thing that ended their sense of being a person with a life of their own.

The question to bring an attorney is not should we get guardianship. It is what specific risks are we trying to prevent, and what is the least restrictive tool that prevents them?

The money trap nobody warns you about

The single most expensive mistake in this whole area, and it is usually made by loving people doing something generous.

Never leave money directly to a person receiving benefits

Means-tested benefits — SSI, and Medicaid with it — have a strict resource limit, historically $2,000 for an individual. Assets above it disqualify the person until the money is spent down.

Which means an inheritance, a life insurance payout, a grandparent's bequest, or a well-meant gift can end the benefits that pay for their housing, health care, and support — and the Medicaid eligibility that came with them is often far harder to get back than it was to lose.

This includes beneficiary designations. Retirement accounts and life insurance pass by designation and override your will entirely. A will that carefully protects them means nothing if a 401(k) form filled out fifteen years ago names them directly.

The structures that solve it

A third-party special needs trust

Funded with other people's money — yours, grandparents', anyone's — for the person's benefit. Assets in it are not counted as theirs, so benefits continue.

  • Set it up now, even if unfunded, so relatives have somewhere to direct gifts and bequests
  • Tell every relative it exists — this is the step most often skipped
  • No Medicaid payback on what's left when the person dies; it can pass to siblings

Why it matters mostThis is the one to establish first. Everything else can be redirected into it.

A first-party special needs trust

Funded with the person's own money — a legal settlement, an inheritance that arrived directly, back benefits.

  • Different rules, and usually a Medicaid payback requirement on death
  • Age limits apply to establishing one
  • A pooled trust run by a nonprofit is often the practical route for smaller amounts

When it comes upUsually as a rescue, after money has already landed in their name. Better to have the third-party trust in place first.

An ABLE account

A tax-advantaged savings account for people whose disability began before a set age. Money in it, up to a substantial threshold, doesn't count against SSI resource limits.

  • The person can control it themselves, which is the real value
  • Spend on housing, transport, education, health, assistive technology, and more
  • Contribution caps apply annually; a working account holder may add more
  • Simple and cheap to open — many states run one, and you usually needn't live there

What it does not replaceA trust. ABLE is for everyday spending and modest savings; a trust is for the larger picture and for what happens after you.

Your will, rewritten

An ordinary will drafted without this in mind is actively dangerous here.

  • Leave their share to the trust, never to them directly
  • Name a trustee, and a successor trustee, and think hard about both
  • Check every beneficiary designation — retirement accounts, life insurance, payable-on-death accounts
  • Tell grandparents and relatives to do the same in theirs
  • Consider life insurance as the funding mechanism if there isn't an estate

The commonest failureA perfect trust that never gets funded, because nobody updated the insurance form.

The letter of intent

Not a legal document, and arguably the most valuable thing you will ever write about your child.

A trust says where the money goes. A letter of intent says who this person is — so that whoever steps in when you cannot has something better than a file.

  • Their history, in your words. Diagnoses, but also what they have been through.
  • How they communicate, including everything a stranger would misread.
  • What a good day looks like, and what a hard one looks like — and what helps in each.
  • Routines that matter, and which ones can move.
  • Food, sleep, medical, sensory. The specifics nobody thinks to write down.
  • Who their people are, and who should stay in their life.
  • What they love. What frightens them. What they have said they want.
  • Your hopes for them, stated plainly, so someone knows what you were aiming at.

Keep it with the trust documents, give a copy to the successor trustee, and update it once a year. The child one-pager builder is a starting point for the practical half of this; the rest is yours to write.

Where an adult lives

The options vary enormously by state and are almost all funded through Medicaid waivers with waiting lists measured in years. Get on lists long before you want a place.

At home, with support coming in

The most common outcome by a wide margin. Personal care attendants, in-home supports, and respite funded through waivers.

Plan forWhat happens when you can no longer do it. This is the arrangement most likely to end in a crisis placement, and least likely to have a written plan.

Shared living or host homes

The person lives with a paid individual or family, often long-term. Can be extraordinary where the match is right.

Ask aboutHow matches are made, what happens if it breaks down, and how the provider is monitored.

Supported or independent living

Their own apartment, with staff hours attached. Ranges from daily drop-in to overnight support.

Ask aboutOvernight coverage, what happens in an emergency, and whether the lease is theirs.

Group homes

Several residents with staff on site. Quality varies enormously between providers and between individual houses run by the same provider.

Visit unannouncedMore than once, at different times of day. Talk to families of current residents, not to the agency's references.

Intermediate care facilities

Larger, medically oriented settings for people with significant medical or behavioral needs.

Weigh carefullyFederal policy strongly favors community settings, and moving out of an institutional placement later can be harder than moving in.

Family and sibling arrangements

A sibling, relative, or friend taking on a role — living together, nearby, or simply overseeing.

Have the conversation earlyExplicitly, with the sibling, without pressure, and long before it is needed. Assumed arrangements are the ones that fail.

Days that are worth having

Where the person actually spends their waking hours matters more to quality of life than almost anything else on this page, and it gets the least planning.

  • Competitive integrated employment — a real job, in the community, at the going wage, alongside people without disabilities. The stated goal of federal policy, and achieved far less often than it should be. Ask for it first.
  • Supported employment — a job coach helping someone get and keep ordinary work. Well evidenced, and often funded through vocational rehabilitation.
  • Customized employment — building a role around what the person can do and what an employer needs, rather than fitting them into an existing job description. Particularly valuable for people with significant support needs.
  • Day habilitation programs — structured group programs. Quality ranges from genuinely engaging to a room and a television. Visit before agreeing to anything.
  • Post-secondary education — inclusive college programs for students with intellectual disability now exist at many institutions, some with federal funding.
  • Volunteering, community groups, and continuing recreation — see adaptive sports and community. Adult programs are scarcer and worth finding early.
  • Vocational rehabilitation — a state agency, separate from everything else, funding assessment, training, equipment, and job support. Apply well before school ends.

Two things worth knowing about work and benefits

Working does not automatically end benefits. There are established work incentive programs that let someone earn while keeping health coverage. Fear of losing benefits keeps many people out of work unnecessarily — ask a benefits counselor before assuming.

Sub-minimum wage employment is being phased out in many places and is contested where it remains. If it is offered, ask what the alternatives are and why they were ruled out.

When you are no longer here

The hardest section to write and the one families most need. Nothing here requires you to be morbid — it requires you to be ordinary about something that is certain.

  • Name a successor trustee, and a successor to them. Consider a professional or corporate trustee alongside a family member, so no one person carries it alone.
  • Separate the money role from the caring role if that suits your family. The person who manages a trust need not be the person who shows up on Sundays.
  • Do not assume a sibling will take over. Ask them, plainly, and give them a real option to say no. Then write down whatever they agree to. Siblings who were assumed into the role, without ever being asked, carry a particular kind of resentment that damages the relationship you were trying to protect.
  • Write the letter of intent, and keep it current.
  • Put the paperwork where it will be found — trust, will, letter of intent, benefit numbers, medical summary, provider contacts, passwords. One place, and tell more than one person where it is.
  • Introduce the successor now. A trustee who has met the person, seen a hard day, and been to an appointment will do the job immeasurably better than one handed a file.
  • Revisit every few years, and after any change in law, family, or the person's needs.

What to do, and when

Age 14 to 16

Transition planning begins

Transition goals enter the IEP. Start visiting adult programs and residential options — years before you need them, purely to know what exists. Open an ABLE account. Apply to the state developmental disability agency for eligibility, which is separate from school and often slow.

Age 16 to 17

Applications and lists

Apply to vocational rehabilitation. Get onto Medicaid waiver waiting lists — this is the single most time-sensitive item on the page, because the lists are years long and your place depends on when you applied. Begin work experience while school still supports it.

Before 18

The legal conversation

See a special needs attorney. Decide on decision-making support and get the documents signed while the person can sign them. Establish the third-party trust and update your will and every beneficiary designation. Tell relatives. Register for Selective Service where required.

At 18

Benefits

Apply for SSI in the month they turn eighteen, even if previously denied — the calculation changes. Apply for Medicaid. Set up representative payee if needed. Confirm educational rights and who holds them.

Two years before school ends

The formal handover

The state transition process begins. Referral is not the same as funded services, and it is subject to appropriation. Push for a specific written plan naming programs, hours, and start dates rather than categories.

The final year

Do not wait for the last month

Confirm what is actually funded, in writing. Have a fallback if it isn't. Build the day: program, work, transport, and what happens in the gaps. The summer after school ends is where families most often fall off a cliff, and it is entirely predictable.

Adulthood, ongoing

Keep it alive

Redetermination for benefits, annual service plan reviews, trust review, and the letter of intent updated. Watch for the person's own preferences changing — this is their life now, and the plan should follow them rather than the other way round.

Before you close this

One thing this week, whatever age your child is: find out whether your state has a Medicaid waiver waiting list, and get on it. It costs nothing, it commits you to nothing, and your position depends entirely on the date you applied. Families who did this at fifteen have options at twenty-two. Families who did it at twenty-two are waiting.

And if your child is small and this page frightened you, close it. Come back when they're thirteen. It will all still be here.
