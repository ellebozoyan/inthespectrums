Cerebral visual impairment:
when the eyes are fine and seeing is not

This page is written to be sent. If somebody has handed you a link because a child in your class, your clinic or your family has CVI, you are in the right place and you do not need any background. It takes about ten minutes.

The one sentence version. The eyes can be perfectly healthy. The difficulty is in how the brain processes what they send, and that can change with tiredness, noise, clutter, novelty and how much else is going on. A child can see something on Monday and genuinely not see it on Thursday, in the same room, with the same eyes.

What it is

Vision is not something the eyes do. The eyes collect light and send signals; roughly a third of the brain is then involved in turning those signals into something usable. Cerebral visual impairment, usually shortened to CVI, is what happens when that second part is affected. The pathways or the processing areas are involved rather than the eye itself.

It is now the leading cause of childhood visual impairment in wealthy countries. It commonly follows prematurity, a period without enough oxygen around birth, a stroke, an infection, seizures, hydrocephalus, or an underlying genetic or metabolic condition, and it often appears alongside cerebral palsy or epilepsy. It also occurs on its own.

An eye exam will usually come back normal, and that is the whole problem. An ophthalmologist checking eye structure and refraction is checking the part that works. Families are told their child's eyes are fine, which is true, and they leave with the original question unanswered.

Why it gets missed for years

Almost always misread as something else first.

It looks like not paying attention

A child who does not find the object, does not look at the speaker, or looks away while listening is read as distractible or uninterested. Looking away can be how they hear you better, because processing both at once may be more than there is room for.

It looks like behavior

Refusing the busy worksheet, melting down in the decorated classroom, freezing in the cafeteria. All reasonable responses to being asked to see in conditions where seeing has become impossible.

It looks like autism

Reduced eye contact, unusual visual behavior, distress in complex environments, strong preferences. The two also genuinely co-occur, which is why one identification does not rule out the other.

It looks inconsistent, so it looks unreal

The single most damaging misreading. A child who sees it once is assumed to be able to see it always, and the days they cannot are treated as a choice. Fluctuation is the condition, not evidence against it.

The result is often years of being described as difficult, lazy, or hard to engage, by adults who were never told there was anything to look for.

The eyes were never the question. Nobody thought to ask a different one.

What people commonly notice

Descriptions families and teachers report, not a checklist and not a test.

  • Finds things far more easily against a plain background than a patterned or crowded one
  • Does better with a single object than with a page, a shelf, or a group
  • Takes a long pause before appearing to see something, then sees it
  • Notices what moves more readily than what is still
  • Reaches without looking, or looks and then reaches, rather than doing both together
  • Uses one part of the visual field far more than another, sometimes turning the head oddly to do it
  • Is drawn to light, or to one particular color, often red or yellow
  • Sees far less when tired, ill, hungry, in pain, or after a long day of holding it together
  • Recognizes a familiar object easily and the same object in a new place not at all
  • Manages in the quiet room and not in the hall, the gym, or the supermarket

Recognizing a child here is a reason to ask somebody, not a conclusion. Several of these appear in other conditions, and none of them identifies CVI on its own.

What tends to help

Most of it is subtraction, and most of it is free.

Reduce what is competing. A plain background, a single item, a cleared surface, a bare wall behind the person speaking. Classroom displays, patterned rugs and busy worksheets are the usual culprits, and they are the usual thing nobody suspects.

Allow the pause. If it takes a while before a child appears to see something, the useful response is to wait rather than to repeat, move, or point again, each of which resets the process. This is one of the most reliably useful adjustments there is and it costs nothing but patience.

One sense at a time. Speaking while a child is trying to look can make looking harder. Say it, then let them look. Or let them look, then say it.

Use what draws them. Movement, backlighting, and a strong single color are often easier to find than a still, matte, multicolored thing. Many families discover their child's color long before anybody tells them it is a known pattern.

Expect the day to matter. Vision at four in the afternoon after a full school day is not vision at nine in the morning. Building the demanding visual tasks into the part of the day where there is capacity is a bigger lever than most equipment.

Things people say that make it easier. Change the words to yours.
  • Take your time. It is still there.
  • I will stop talking while you look.
  • I put it on the plain mat so it is easier to find.
  • You found it yesterday and not today. That is the CVI, not you.

Who assesses it, and what it opens

Identification usually involves neuro-ophthalmology, developmental optometry, or a CVI-specific functional vision assessment, often alongside a teacher of students with visual impairments. An ordinary eye exam still matters, because ocular conditions and CVI often occur together and each gets missed in the presence of the other.

In the United States, an identification of visual impairment establishes eligibility under IDEA. That can open a teacher of students with visual impairments, orientation and mobility instruction, the expanded core curriculum, and services from a state commission for the blind. Those services are the reason identification matters practically as well as personally.

What is settled and what is not

Settled: that CVI is brain-based, that it is common, that ordinary eye exams do not find it, that visual function fluctuates with fatigue and complexity, and that reducing visual clutter helps.

Actively debated: how CVI should be assessed and scored, which intervention frameworks work best, and how the boundaries of the diagnosis should be drawn. There is more than one established approach and their proponents disagree in good faith. If somebody tells you there is one correct method, that is a stronger claim than the field currently supports.

What follows from that: ask what approach a professional uses and why, and treat a confident single answer with more caution than a hedged one.

Where to read something better than this

Perkins School for the Blind publishes CVI Now, which is the strongest current family-facing material on this and is free.

The Center for Parent Information and Resources, funded by the US Department of Education, publishes a free fact sheet, Visual Impairment, Including Blindness, at parentcenterhub.org. It is public domain, written for families, and available in Spanish. The National Eye Institute at the National Institutes of Health is the federal source on vision conditions generally, and MedlinePlus at medlineplus.gov will point you to whichever institute covers a given question.

Your state commission for the blind and the American Printing House for the Blind are where materials, equipment and services actually come from.

Neither those organizations nor any government agency is the source of what is written here, and nothing on this page is endorsed by them. Where this page and any of them disagree on a matter of fact, they are the ones to trust.

Before you close this

If you are a teacher, an aide, a therapist or a relative who was sent this link, the useful thing you can do is small: clear the background, wait longer than feels natural, and stop talking while they look. Those three cost nothing and they change what a child can do in your room.

If you are the person doing the sending, you have just explained in ten minutes something that took you a great deal longer to work out, and you did it without having to be in the room while somebody read it.

Nothing here is medical or educational advice. It is an overview meant to help you understand the shape of something and ask better questions of the people who can assess a particular child.
