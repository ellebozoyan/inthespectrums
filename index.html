The name is plural, and the preposition is deliberate

On the spectrum is a phrase many people use with real warmth, and it has helped families find each other. It does, though, describe a line. One dimension, with people placed somewhere along it. We wanted a name that opened outward instead. One with room enough for everybody.

A life is far more than a single spectrum. There are dozens — how you sense, move, sleep, eat, communicate, feel, attend, dream, and find joy, and every one of us is somewhere in all of them at once. Nobody sits in the middle of all of them. We are not points on a line. We are surfing the waves of a life, carrying real burdens and holding real gifts, moving all the time — inside and out.

What differs between people is what a particular position needs, has recognized, asks for, and actually gets, and how much of the gap between what someone needs and what the world offers has been closed so far. That gap is something people build, not something that simply happens to us, which means it moves when we move it.

Nobody has to qualify. A lisp counts. So does not being able to hop, or hating the seam in a sock, or handwriting nobody can read, or eating six foods and no others, or standing at the edge of the playground every day. And so does a family carrying fifteen of these at once. That second group is the one almost nothing else is written for.

How this site is written

Stated plainly, so you know what you are reading and can weigh it accordingly.

It says where the evidence is contested

Several topics here are genuinely disputed among clinicians — behavioral therapy approaches, tick-borne illness, post-infectious syndromes, tongue-tie release, airway orthodontics. This site describes the disagreement and where each side stands rather than choosing for you. Be cautious of anyone certain when the evidence isn't.

It offers options where families reasonably differ

How you talk to your child about their own life, what you disclose and to whom, how you frame difference — these are values decisions, not clinical ones. Where that's true, you'll find approaches with reasoning attached instead of instructions.

It presumes competence, always

Comprehension usually runs ahead of expression. Nonspeaking is not non-thinking. Everything here is written on the assumption that the child being discussed is listening and understands more than anyone has measured.

It's free, and it's yours to change

No login, no ads, no sponsors, nothing sold. Take the templates, rewrite them, put your own name on them. These are here to back you up, so that when you walk into the meeting, the appointment, or the classroom, you arrive prepared, in your own words, and impossible to overlook.

It goes out of date, and says so

Program names, billing codes, legal timelines, and clinical guidance all drift. Pages are dated and reviewed, and every one tells you to verify specifics locally. Where a detail matters, check it against your own state and your own plan.

On language

Person-first language is the house style — a child with autism, a person with a disability. Many adults with autism, and many Deaf people, prefer identity-first language for themselves, and that preference is about identity rather than etiquette. Where a person tells you what they want to be called, that outranks any style guide, including this one.

Who this is for

  • Families at any stage — the first week of worrying, the tenth year of it, or somewhere in between.
  • Teachers and paraprofessionals with a new student whose file is forty pages long. Start with the accommodations finder, then the conditions library.
  • Therapists — speech, occupational, physical, behavioral, mental health — wanting to get oriented quickly outside your own discipline, or wanting something to hand a family that won't send them somewhere alarming.
  • Physicians and nurses who have four minutes left in an appointment and need to point a family somewhere solid. Most pages are written to be read cold, without the rest of the site.
  • Grandparents, babysitters, coaches, and everyone else who loves a child and wants to get it right. The child one-pager was built for you.
  • Anyone raising a child at all. Most of what's here — how to talk about difference, how to read together, how to keep a child safe — is not specialized. It's just written more carefully than usual.
  • Whatever the size of it. One lisp, or one child who cannot hop, or one who eats six things. You do not need a diagnosis to be in the right place. And if your family is carrying layer upon layer at once, this was built for you especially, because almost nothing else is.
