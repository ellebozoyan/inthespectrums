The Conditions Library:
what it is, how widely it varies, and what helps

Every entry here answers the same four questions: what this actually is, how much it differs from one person to the next, what genuinely helps, and what commonly gets missed. None of it diagnoses anyone.

A note on the four groupings. They exist so you can find things, not to say anything about where a condition truly belongs. Almost everything here sits in more than one — CVI is neurological in origin and appears under access because that is where its effects land; anxiety is as physical as it is mental; sleep sits underneath nearly everything else. Where the overlap changes what you would actually do, the entry says so.
A note on language. This site uses person-first language — a child with autism — as its house style. Many adults with autism, and many Deaf people, prefer identity-first language for themselves — “autistic person,” “Deaf person.” That preference is not a matter of etiquette but of identity. Where a person or community tells you what they want to be called, that wins over any style guide, including this one.

Reading this as a professional

Written to be readable across disciplines, so an SLP can get oriented in a genetics question and a teacher can get oriented in a medical one.

The section most worth your time is what commonly gets missed — it's where the errors made by well-meaning people outside their own specialty tend to sit.

One last thing

A label tells a clinician where to start looking. It never explains your child.

Whatever word brought you to this page, you will still know things about them that no summary and no evaluation will ever capture — how they wake up, what settles them, what they are like on a good day. That knowledge is what makes every plan on this site work, and you are the only person who has it.

This is a map, not a diagnostic tool and not medical advice. Descriptions here are general and simplified. Real people don't match summaries, co-occurrence is the rule rather than the exception, and only a qualified clinician who has met your child can diagnose anything.

Where a condition is contested, disputed, or not formally recognized, this library says so rather than choosing a side for you.
