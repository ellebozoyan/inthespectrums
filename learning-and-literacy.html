Learning and literacy:
reading is taught, not caught

Difficulty with reading, writing, or math is among the most common reasons a child struggles at school, and among the most fixable — provided the instruction is the right kind. The evidence here is unusually strong and unusually often ignored, which means knowing what to ask for matters more on this topic than on almost any other.

Reading this as a professional

This page describes structured literacy and names the instructional approaches that research has not supported, because families are often told their child's program is evidence-based when it isn't.

The section on evaluating a school's reading instruction is written to be usable by a parent in a meeting, and may be useful if you are ever explaining your own program to one.

What reading is actually made of

Reading comprehension is not one skill. It is two, and they multiply rather than add, which means if either one is near zero, the result is too, however strong the other is.

DecodingTurning marks on a page into the sounds of words. A code, and codes are taught.

Language comprehensionUnderstanding those words when spoken — vocabulary, grammar, background knowledge.

Reading comprehensionUnderstanding what was written.

This is why two children can both be failing at reading for completely opposite reasons. One decodes fluently and understands almost nothing of what they read. Another understands every word when it's read aloud and cannot get the letters off the page. They need entirely different help, and a school that treats them the same will fail at least one of them. The first question is always which side is the problem on, or is it both?

Structured literacy: what actually works

The approach with the strongest evidence base, and the one to ask for by name.

Structured literacy teaches the code explicitly, in a planned order, with enough practice for each step to stick before the next arrives. It is not one branded program — it's a set of features that good programs share.

  • Explicit. Each element is directly taught rather than discovered. Nothing is left for a child to infer.
  • Systematic and cumulative. Skills are sequenced from simple to complex, and each builds on what came before.
  • Phonemic awareness first. Hearing and manipulating the individual sounds inside spoken words — before letters are involved at all. This is the single strongest predictor of later reading, and the most commonly skipped.
  • Phonics, taught in an order. Letter-sound relationships taught deliberately, not incidentally.
  • Decodable text at the start. Early books that use only patterns the child has been taught, so reading is practice rather than guesswork.
  • Multisensory. Seeing, saying, hearing, and moving together — tracing letters while saying sounds, tapping out phonemes.
  • Diagnostic and responsive. The teacher knows exactly which sounds and patterns a child has and hasn't mastered, and reteaches accordingly.
  • Morphology and syntax as reading advances — prefixes, roots, suffixes, sentence structure. Not just sounds.

The thing families are rarely told

Structured literacy is not remedial or specialized. It is simply how reading is most reliably taught, and it works for children who would have learned to read anyway. What differs for a child with dyslexia is the intensity — more explicit, more practice, more repetition, smaller group, not the method.

Which means a school saying "we don't do that here, we do it differently" is not describing a philosophical alternative. It is describing a program that is likely to leave some children behind.

How to tell what your school is actually doing

The word "evidence-based" appears on nearly every curriculum. These questions get past it.

Ask these, and listen carefully

  • "What reading program do you use, by name?" Then look it up independently. Reviews of program quality are published and freely available.
  • "Is phonics taught systematically, in a set sequence, or as it comes up?" As-it-comes-up is incidental phonics, and it is not the same thing.
  • "Do beginning readers get decodable books?" If early books are predictable or pattern-based, children memorize and guess rather than decode.
  • "When my child hits a word they don't know, what are they taught to do?" This is the most revealing question on the list. See below.
  • "How do you know which specific sounds my child hasn't mastered?" A good answer involves diagnostic assessment, not a reading level.
  • "How much time per day, in what group size, with whom?" Intensity is what makes intervention work.

The answer that tells you the most

If a child stuck on a word is taught to look at the picture, guess from context, or think about what word would make sense, that is a cueing strategy, and it teaches children to avoid the code rather than use it. It works passably in simple early books and collapses around third grade when the text gets harder and the pictures disappear.

The answer you want is that they are taught to look at the letters and sound it out, using patterns they have been explicitly taught.

Curricula built around guessing strategies were widely used for decades and have been substantially withdrawn or revised following sustained criticism. Some schools are still using them, and many teachers were trained in them in good faith. Asking this question is not an accusation, but the answer tells you what your child is being taught to do.

Check the eyes and ears first

Before reading difficulty is attributed to anything, to dyslexia, to attention, to motivation, or to still learning the language.

This matters most for a multilingual child

When a child is learning a new language, almost anything can be explained away by it. Not responding when called. Mishearing words. Losing the place on the page. Avoiding books. Copying wrongly from the board.

Every one of those is also what uncorrected vision or hearing looks like, and a child who is still learning the language cannot usually tell you which it is.

So do it in this order: rule out the eyes and ears, then talk about language. Not because it is likely, but because it is cheap, quick, treatable, and the alternative is years of the wrong explanation.

What a school screening actually covers

Less than most families assume, and it is designed that way — screening is meant to catch the obvious and refer on, not to assess anybody.

  • Vision screening usually checks distance acuity, sometimes near acuity. A child can pass it with 20/20 eyesight and still be unable to read comfortably.
  • What it does not check: focusing (holding clear vision up close as the page is worked through), eye teaming (the two eyes working together), and tracking (moving accurately along a line and to the next one). These are precisely the skills reading demands, and difficulty with them produces eye strain, headaches, double or blurred vision, losing the place, and avoiding the task, with normal acuity throughout.
  • Fatigue is part of it. A child may see well in a two-minute screening at nine in the morning and be struggling badly by the afternoon. A screening cannot detect that; a parent and teacher can.
  • Hearing screening usually checks pure tones in a quiet room. It can miss fluctuating hearing loss from fluid, mild loss, one-sided loss, and — importantly — difficulty listening in a noisy classroom, which is where a child actually has to do it.

The three levels, and when each applies

Screening

At school or a routine well visit. Quick, free, and designed to catch the obvious.

  • Distance vision, basic hearing tones
  • Pass means nothing obvious was found
  • It does not mean vision or hearing are fine

Its jobTo find who needs a real exam. Nothing more.

A full examination

An eye examination with an optometrist or ophthalmologist; an audiological evaluation with an audiologist.

  • Eye health, refraction, and. Ask for it specifically — focusing, teaming and tracking
  • Full audiogram, middle ear function, and speech understanding in noise
  • This is where most reading-related vision and hearing problems are actually found

Worth asking"Can you assess binocular vision and accommodation?" and "Can we test listening in background noise?"

A functional assessment

A different question: not how well the eyes or ears work, but how this person uses them in a real room, on a real day.

  • Done by a teacher of the visually impaired, a certified orientation and mobility specialist, or an educational audiologist
  • Conducted where the child actually is — classroom, hallway, playground
  • Produces educational recommendations rather than a prescription
  • Usually the route to vision or hearing services in an IEP

NoteLargely observational, so it depends far less on language than a standard test, which is an advantage for a multilingual child.

So which should you ask for?

Not instead of, in order. A functional assessment is not a substitute for a proper examination, and asking for one first usually means waiting longer for something a straightforward eye exam would have found.

Start with a full examination. Refractive error and middle ear fluid are common, treatable, and account for a great deal of unexplained reading difficulty.

Then ask for a functional assessment if any of these are true:

  • The examination came back normal, but the child behaves as though they cannot see or hear well
  • Function varies a lot by environment, time of day, or fatigue
  • There is a neurological reason — brain injury, prematurity, seizures, a genetic condition, or suspected CVI, where the eyes are healthy and the difficulty is in the brain
  • Standardized testing keeps producing results that do not match what you see at home
  • You are seeking vision or hearing support in an IEP, where a functional assessment is generally what the team needs

You can request either in writing through school, and an outside evaluation is also an option. See inside the IEP.

One caution on claims: you will see a striking statistic quoted about what proportion of learning is visual. It is repeated widely and does not have solid evidence behind it. Vision matters for reading. That case does not need a number that will not survive being looked up.

Growing up with more than one language

A common source of unnecessary worry, unnecessary delay, and occasionally both at once.

Two things that are both true

Learning more than one language does not cause language delay, reading difficulty, or a learning disability. It is not too much for a child, it does not confuse them, and the research on this is not close. Families are still told otherwise routinely, including by professionals.

And bilingualism does not protect a child from anything either. A child learning two languages can also have dyslexia, a language disorder, hearing loss or anything else, at the same rate as anybody. Which is why "it is just the two languages" is a dangerous thing to accept without checking.

What is normal, and gets mistaken for a problem

  • A smaller vocabulary in each language, taken separately. Counted together, across both, the total is typically comparable to a monolingual child — the words are simply distributed across two systems. Testing in one language alone will make a perfectly typical child look delayed.
  • Mixing languages within one sentence. This is a normal feature of bilingual speech, follows grammatical rules, and is done by fluent adult bilinguals everywhere. It is not confusion.
  • A silent period when a new language starts, sometimes lasting months. Common, and not the same as losing skills.
  • Different strengths in different settings. One language for home and feelings, another for school and academic work. That is how bilingualism works, and it is a sign the system is doing its job.
  • An accent, or grammatical errors that persist. Neither predicts reading ability.

The gap that causes the most trouble at school

Conversational fluency in a new language arrives in roughly one to two years. The academic language needed to read a textbook, follow a lesson and write an essay takes much longer — commonly five to seven years, and longer again for a child who is also learning to read.

A child who chats easily in the playground is therefore assumed to be fully proficient, and then judged against that assumption in the classroom. Sounding fluent and being able to learn in a language are different things, and the gap between them is where plenty of children get written off as unmotivated.

Telling a language difference from a language disorder

This is the question that matters, and there is a clear principle.

  • A true disorder shows up in every language the child speaks. Difficulty only in the newer language is a language difference. Difficulty in the home language too — judged by the standards of that language, by somebody who speaks it — points to a disorder.
  • Ask specifically: how does their language compare with siblings and cousins who learned the same way, in the same home?
  • Assessment should involve both languages. Testing only in the school language over-identifies children who are simply still learning it, and under-identifies those who need help.
  • Ask what the evaluator did about language. An interpreter is better than nothing, though it is not the same as an assessment designed for bilingual children. It is fair to ask, and to have the answer written into the report.
  • Do not wait years to find out. The most common harm here is delay — a real difficulty attributed to bilingualism, monitored, and left until third or fourth grade when intervention is considerably harder.

Do not drop the home language

Families are still advised to speak only the school language at home, usually with good intentions. The evidence does not support it, and the costs are real.

A strong home language supports the second rather than competing with it — concepts and reading skills transfer across languages. Meanwhile a child who loses the home language loses conversation with grandparents, access to their own family's stories, and part of how they belong somewhere.

If a professional advises dropping it, it is entirely reasonable to ask what evidence that is based on.

Reading instruction itself does not change: structured literacy works for multilingual learners as it does for everyone, with more explicit attention to vocabulary and background knowledge. What changes is the interpretation, not the method.

The specific difficulties

Dyslexia

Difficulty with accurate and fluent word recognition, and with spelling and decoding — typically stemming from a weakness in processing the sounds of language, and unexpected given the person's other abilities.

  • Trouble rhyming, hearing syllables, and isolating sounds in words
  • Slow, effortful, inaccurate reading
  • Spelling much weaker than spoken vocabulary
  • Reading comprehension better when listening than when reading
  • Family history is common

What you'd see at homeHomework taking three times as long, stomachaches before reading, avoiding books entirely, reading a word correctly on one line and not the next.

Dysgraphia

Difficulty with the physical and organizational demands of writing. Frequently overlooked, because it looks like carelessness.

  • Handwriting that is slow, effortful, or illegible
  • Hand pain and fatigue while writing
  • Written work far below what the person can say aloud
  • Difficulty organizing thoughts on a page
  • Trouble with spacing, sizing, and staying on lines

What you'd see at homeRefusing to write anything, a page of work that took an hour, ideas that vanish between thinking and writing.

Dyscalculia

Difficulty with number sense, math facts, and calculation — the least recognized of the three, and rarely identified without a parent pushing for it.

  • Poor sense of quantity, which number is bigger, roughly how many
  • Math facts that never become automatic despite endless practice
  • Counting on fingers well past the age peers stop
  • Difficulty with time, money, sequencing, and estimation
  • Math anxiety that arrives early and runs deep

What you'd see at homeTears over math homework, memorizing facts and losing them overnight, no idea whether an answer is roughly right.

Language-based comprehension difficulty

The other side of the equation. A person decodes accurately and does not understand what they've read, usually because the underlying spoken language is weaker than anyone realized.

  • Reads aloud fluently, can't say what it was about
  • Limited vocabulary and background knowledge
  • Difficulty with inference and figurative language
  • Often overlaps with developmental language disorder

What you'd see at homeBeing praised as a good reader while grades slide, and struggling with instructions and conversation too.

Fluency without accuracy, or the reverse

Fluency is the bridge. A person who decodes accurately but very slowly uses so much effort on the words that nothing is left for meaning.

  • Accurate but painfully slow reading
  • Word-by-word reading with no expression
  • Comprehension collapsing on longer texts specifically
  • Repeated reading and partner reading are the usual interventions

What you'd see at homeGetting through a page correctly and remembering none of it, and being exhausted by short assignments.

Executive function in the middle of all of it

Getting started, holding a plan, tracking materials, and finishing. Difficulty here can look exactly like a reading or math problem and needs a different response.

  • Knows the material, can't produce the assignment
  • Loses work between completing it and handing it in
  • Freezes at the start of an open-ended task
  • Time and long-range planning are the hardest parts

See alsoThe ADHD and executive function page covers this in full.

Accommodation and instruction are both required

A common and costly mistake is choosing one.

Instruction is the work of building the skill — structured literacy, delivered intensively enough to close a gap. Accommodation is the work of keeping a person's access to everything else while the skill is being built.

A child given only accommodations may reach adulthood unable to read. A child given only instruction spends years locked out of history, science, and literature because they can't access the text. They need both, at the same time, and asking for one should never mean giving up the other.

Accommodations that preserve access

  • Audiobooks and text-to-speech for all grade-level content, provided at the same time as peers get the printed version.
  • Speech-to-text and keyboarding where handwriting is the barrier, with real instruction in using them.
  • Extended time as standard, and reduced volume where a task repeats a mastered skill.
  • Not being asked to read aloud in front of the class unless they volunteer.
  • Grading content separately from spelling and mechanics, except where those are what's being assessed.
  • Calculators and reference sheets for math facts, so a fact-retrieval problem doesn't block conceptual learning.

Documenting early matters more than families expect

Accommodations used consistently through school are what establish the record required for accommodations on college entrance exams and in higher education. A student who first requests extended time at sixteen, with no history of it, is in a much harder position than one who has had it written and used since third grade.

So even where a family is managing without a formal plan, there is a real argument for getting it documented anyway.

Evidence, sorted honestly

Strongly supported

Explicit, systematic phonics instruction. One of the most replicated findings in education research.

Phonemic awareness training, particularly in the earliest years.

Early intervention. Kindergarten and first grade instruction is dramatically more effective than the same instruction in fourth grade. The gap widens every year it isn't addressed.

Repeated reading for fluency.

Explicit vocabulary and background knowledge instruction for comprehension.

Assistive technology — audiobooks and text-to-speech preserve access to content and do not reduce reading motivation.

Explicit, structured math instruction with concrete-to-abstract sequencing.

Mixed or context-dependent

Specific branded programs. Many quality programs share the same underlying features; the features matter more than the brand. Some heavily marketed programs have thinner evidence than their reputation.

Multisensory elements specifically — widely used and clinically sensible; the evidence that the multisensory component adds beyond good structured instruction is less clear.

Fluency practice for comprehension — helps some readers substantially, others less.

Not supported. Ask hard questions

Three-cueing and guessing strategies — using pictures, context, or first letters to predict a word. This is the central instructional problem in reading, and it teaches avoidance of the code.

Predictable and pattern books as beginning readers, which reward memorization over decoding.

Colored overlays and tinted lenses for dyslexia.

Vision therapy, eye exercises, and eye tracking training as a treatment for dyslexia — distinct from treating a genuine diagnosed vision problem, which is a separate and legitimate matter.

Working memory training programs marketed to improve reading.

Primitive reflex integration, auditory training, and balance programs offered as reading interventions.

At each stage

Preschool

Sounds, before letters

Rhyming, clapping syllables, playing with the sounds inside words, and being read to constantly. Difficulty with rhyme and sound awareness at four or five is the earliest reliable signal there is, and the cheapest moment to act.

Kindergarten and first grade

The window

Explicit phonics, decodable texts, and daily practice. Intervention here is far more effective than at any later point. If a child is behind at the end of first grade, act now rather than waiting to see — waiting is what turns a gap into a chasm.

Second to fourth grade

Where it gets visible

Text becomes harder, pictures disappear, and children who were coping by guessing hit a wall. This is when many families are first told there's a problem, and when compensating readers stop compensating. Accommodations become essential alongside continued instruction.

Middle school

Content, self-knowledge, and self-esteem

Reading to learn rather than learning to read. Assistive technology becomes central, and so does the young person understanding their own profile well enough to explain it. Protecting self-esteem is a real goal here, not a soft one.

High school and beyond

Independence and the paper trail

Self-advocacy, technology they own and can use anywhere, and the documentation required for college entrance exams and higher education accommodations. Start the documentation conversation two years before it's needed.

Recognizing good instruction

Signs it's working

  • Phonics is taught in a stated sequence, and someone can tell you where your child is in it
  • Beginning readers get decodable books
  • Progress is measured in specific skills, not reading levels
  • Intervention is daily, small-group or individual, and delivered by someone trained in the program
  • Accommodations run alongside instruction rather than instead of it
  • Your child can tell you what they're working on
  • The word dyslexia is used freely, without discomfort
  • Grade-level content is still reaching them through audio while reading is being built

Reasons to push

  • "He's just a late bloomer" or "let's give it another year"
  • Reading level is the only measure anyone can produce
  • Your child is taught to guess from pictures or context
  • Intervention is twice a week in a group of eight
  • The program name changes each year, or nobody can name it
  • Accommodations offered in place of instruction
  • Reluctance to use the word dyslexia
  • Homework rising because school time isn't working

On the word itself

Some districts avoid saying dyslexia, and families are sometimes told the term can't be used in a school setting. The US Department of Education has issued guidance clarifying that there is nothing preventing schools from using dyslexia, dysgraphia, and dyscalculia in evaluations and IEP documents.

You can use the word. So can they.

Before you close this

One question, at the next school meeting: when my child comes to a word they don't know, what are they taught to do?

The answer takes ten seconds and tells you most of what you need. And if the answer isn't look at the letters and sound it out, you now know exactly what to ask for, which is a lot more than most parents have when they walk into that room.

This is a map, not an evaluation. Identifying a specific learning disorder requires proper assessment by a qualified evaluator, and instructional decisions belong to teams who know your child. Program availability and state requirements vary.
