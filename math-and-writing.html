Math and writing:
what is actually going wrong

The two subjects children are most often called lazy about. Both are built from many separate skills stacked together, which means failure at the top can come from a gap almost anywhere underneath — and the gap is often not in the subject at all.

Reading has its own page. See learning and literacy for decoding, phonics, comprehension and dyslexia. This page covers the other two things school grades.

Math is a stack

Each layer rests on the one below. A gap low down breaks everything above it, and the break shows up years later looking like something else.

  • Number sense — knowing what a quantity is. That seven is more than five without counting, that numbers sit in order along a line, that four objects are four however they are arranged. This develops before school and is where dyscalculia usually lives.
  • Counting and correspondence — one number per object, the last number said is the answer, the total does not change if you start from the other end.
  • Facts — knowing that 6 + 7 is 13 without working it out. Not for its own sake, but because a working memory occupied with calculating 6 + 7 has nothing left for the actual problem.
  • Procedures — the steps of long multiplication, borrowing, fractions.
  • Application — word problems, multi-step reasoning, deciding which operation the situation needs.

Why this matters for what you do next

A child struggling with fractions in fourth grade may have a fractions problem. Far more often they have an unaddressed gap two or three layers down — shaky number sense, or facts that still have to be counted out every time — and the fractions are simply where it became impossible to hide.

Which is why more fractions practice frequently does nothing. The useful question is not "what are they failing" but "where did it stop being solid".

Dyscalculia

  • Roughly as common as dyslexia, and identified far less often. Most schools screen for reading difficulty and almost none screen for this, so it is usually found late — if at all.
  • The core difficulty is number sense, not arithmetic. Quantities do not feel like anything. Comparing two numbers, estimating, or judging whether an answer is roughly right all require deliberate effort rather than intuition.
  • Signs worth noticing: still counting on fingers well past when peers stopped; no sense of whether an answer is reasonable; difficulty with time, money and estimating; forgetting a fact learned yesterday; anxiety that is specific to math rather than general.
  • It is not a general intelligence issue, and it commonly sits alongside strong verbal ability — which is exactly why it gets read as not trying.
  • It can also co-occur with dyslexia and ADHD, and often does.

Before assuming it is math

A great many children fail math for reasons that are not mathematical. Check these first.

  • Reading. A word problem is a reading task with a calculation buried in it. If oral versions are much easier, the difficulty is in the words.
  • Working memory. Multi-step problems require holding the intermediate result while doing the next step. Something that vanishes mid-problem looks like a math failure and is not.
  • Processing speed and timed tests. A person who knows the material and cannot produce it in ninety seconds will score as though they do not know it.
  • Vision. Columns misaligned, digits transposed, place value lost — see check the eyes and ears first.
  • Handwriting. If forming the digits is effortful, the effort is coming out of the math.
  • Missed instruction. Math is more sequential than any other subject. Weeks absent, or an unfinished year, leaves gaps that never close on their own.
  • Anxiety. Which is next, and is not a small factor.

Math anxiety is measurable, and it is often manufactured

Anxiety about math actively consumes working memory — the same working memory the problem needs. So a frightened person performs worse than their actual ability, then concludes they are bad at math, which produces more fear. It is a genuine mechanism, not an excuse.

Timed tests are the most reliable way to produce it. Speed drills under public pressure teach a substantial number of children that math is a thing they fail at, and the fluency they buy is available by other routes.

Adults saying "I was never any good at math either" — kindly meant — also transmits it. So does hovering over homework anxiously.

What actually helps

  • Concrete, then pictures, then symbols. Physical objects first, then a drawing of them, then the numbers. Skipping to symbols is where most people lose it, and manipulatives are not babyish at any age — mathematicians use models constantly.
  • Explicit, systematic, cumulative teaching — the same principles that work for reading. Show it, do it together, then alone, with each thing building on the last.
  • Build fact fluency without pressure. Games, short daily practice, strategies rather than rote recall. Fluency is worth having; the timed test is not the only way to get it.
  • Fill the gap you find, even if it is years back. Unglamorous and far more effective than pushing on with grade-level content nobody can access.
  • Let them talk it through. Explaining reasoning aloud reveals precisely where it breaks, which a wrong answer alone does not.
  • Estimate first, then calculate. Builds the sense of whether an answer is reasonable, which is the thing dyscalculia lacks most.
  • Allow a calculator where the goal is the reasoning. It does not prevent learning — it removes a barrier to the part actually being taught.
  • Reduce the visual load — fewer problems per page, more space, graph paper for alignment.

Writing is the heaviest task in school

Not because the ideas are hard, but because it demands almost everything at once.

Producing a paragraph requires, simultaneously: thinking of something to say, holding it in mind while writing, choosing words, building sentences, spelling, punctuating, forming letters or finding keys, organizing the whole, and monitoring all of it as you go.

Every one of those competes for the same limited capacity. Which is why a person who can tell you a rich, detailed story out loud may hand in three flat sentences — and why the gap between what they know and what appears on paper is the single most useful thing to notice.

The test that tells you most

Ask them to say it instead. Record it, scribe it, let them dictate to a phone.

If the spoken version is dramatically better than the written one — and it very often is — the difficulty is not in the thinking, the knowledge, or the effort. It is somewhere in the machinery of getting it onto paper, and that is a completely different problem with completely different solutions.

It is also worth doing simply so somebody sees what they actually know, having possibly never had that recognized.

Dysgraphia, and what it covers

  • Handwriting difficulty — letter formation, spacing, staying on the line, speed, and pain or fatigue after a short time. Often with an awkward grip and unusual posture.
  • Written expression difficulty — organizing ideas on paper, which can occur with perfectly neat handwriting.
  • They can occur separately or together, and the supports differ, so Know about which you are dealing with.
  • Effortful handwriting takes capacity from everything else. A person spending their attention on forming letters has less left for what the letters are supposed to say.
  • Pain is common and rarely reported. Ask directly whether their hand hurts, because many assume it does for everybody.

Separating the loads

Almost every effective support does the same thing: it takes one job off the pile so the others have room.

  • Plan before writing. Talk it through, list, or map it — so organizing and composing are not happening at the same moment.
  • Dictate the first draft. Speech-to-text, or an adult scribing. Editing what exists is far easier than producing from nothing.
  • Teach typing properly and early. For many people this is transformative, and it is treated as an afterthought far too often.
  • Word prediction and spellcheck, so spelling does not interrupt composition mid-sentence.
  • Separate the drafts. Get it out first, fix it second. Trying to write and correct simultaneously stalls most people.
  • Reduce volume where volume is not the point. Three good sentences demonstrate understanding as well as a page does.
  • Give a structure — a sentence starter, a paragraph frame, a worked example. Not a crutch: it is how everybody learns a form.

The grading problem

Do not grade content on mechanics. When spelling and handwriting are marked in the same number as understanding, a person who knows the material perfectly receives a score saying they do not — and, over years, comes to believe it.

Ask for them to be assessed separately. Knowledge of the subject, and accuracy of the writing, are two different things to know about a student, and combining them tells you neither.

Written accommodations — extra time, a scribe, speech-to-text, reduced volume, alternative formats — belong in an IEP or 504 plan. See inside the IEP and the accommodations finder.

Handwriting: teach it or bypass it

A real decision, and worth making deliberately rather than by default.

  • Explicit handwriting instruction works, particularly when it is early, brief, daily and taught by formation pattern rather than alphabetical order. An occupational therapist can advise.
  • There is a point at which effort is better spent elsewhere. If years of practice have not produced usable, comfortable writing, continuing to drill it costs time that typing and dictation would repay quickly.
  • It is not either-or. Keep enough handwriting for signatures and short notes; move the volume to a keyboard.
  • Do not withhold typing until handwriting is fixed. This is common and it costs children years of written expression they could have been developing.

What about everything else

Briefly, because the answer is usually the same.

Difficulty in science, history and most other subjects is nearly always downstream of one of three things: reading the material, writing the answers, or the executive load of a long assignment. Support those and the subject usually follows.

Which is worth saying to a school. "He is failing history" often means he cannot read the textbook, cannot produce an essay, or cannot manage a three-week project — and none of those is a history problem.

Before you close this

If one subject is going badly, the most useful question is rarely "how do we get better at it". It is "which of the many things this task requires is the one giving way" — because that is what you can actually do something about.

And ask them to say it out loud. What comes back is often nothing like what has been appearing on paper.

Nothing here is educational or clinical advice. A persistent difficulty in one subject warrants proper assessment rather than more of the same practice.
