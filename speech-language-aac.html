Speech, language and AAC

Speech-language pathology covers far more than speech sounds. Understanding, expressing, socializing, reading, swallowing, and, for a great many children — communicating in a form that isn't speech at all. This page covers the direct work and the augmentative work as one discipline, because for most children they belong together from the start.

Reading this as a professional

Written for families and for colleagues in other disciplines. The AAC section is the most substantial, because it is the area where family understanding most often lags and where a poorly implemented system fails for reasons that have nothing to do with the device.

The evidence section names several popular approaches whose research base is thinner than their reputation, and one area where professional bodies have taken a clear position. Both may be useful when a family arrives having read something online.

What communication is actually for

Almost every system starts with requesting, and plenty of never move past it. But requesting is one function out of many, and it is the only one that serves the adult as much as the child.

RefusingNo. Stop. Not that one. I don't want to
CommentingLook at that. That's weird. I like this
QuestioningWhat is it? Where are we going? Why?
GreetingHi. Bye. I'm here. I'm leaving
ProtestingThat's not fair. You're wrong. Stop doing that
Sharing feelingI'm scared. I'm excited. That hurt
NarratingTelling what happened, to someone who wasn't there
JokingBeing funny on purpose, being silly with someone
NegotiatingFive more minutes. Can I do it later instead?
DirectingCome here. Do it this way. Watch me

This is the test to apply to any therapy plan or any device. Can this child refuse, comment, question, joke, complain, and tell someone they're wrong? A system that only produces requests teaches a child that communication is a vending machine, and leaves them unable to say the sentences that matter most.

What speech-language therapy targets

Nine areas. Most children work on two or three, and the balance between them shifts a lot as they grow.

Understanding language

The receptive side, and the one most often overestimated, because a child who talks fluently is assumed to follow everything.

  • Vocabulary and concept understanding
  • Following directions of increasing length and complexity
  • Grammar comprehension — who did what to whom
  • Processing time and processing under noise or fatigue
  • Inference, figurative language, and what wasn't said outright

What you'd see at homeDoing the last part of a three-part instruction, "what?" as a reflex, behavior falling apart during language-heavy parts of the day, following better one-to-one than in a group.

Using language

The expressive side — vocabulary, sentence structure, and being able to get an idea across the way it was intended.

  • Vocabulary growth, including words that aren't nouns
  • Sentence length, grammar, and word order
  • Word finding and retrieval
  • Narrative — telling a story in an order someone else can follow
  • Repair strategies when the listener doesn't understand

What you'd see at homeShort sentences past the expected age, heavy reliance on "thing" and "stuff," stories that jump around, giving up when misunderstood.

Speech sounds

Producing the sounds of a language accurately enough to be understood by people who don't know the child.

  • Articulation of individual sounds
  • Phonological patterns — whole classes of sounds affected together
  • Intelligibility to familiar and unfamiliar listeners
  • Carryover into real conversation, which is where most of the work is

What you'd see at homeFamily understands and strangers don't, frustration when not understood, avoiding speaking to unfamiliar people, being asked to repeat constantly. Where errors follow a pattern rather than landing on single sounds, see phonological disorders; where structure may be limiting movement, see myofunctional therapy.

Motor speech

When the difficulty is planning and sequencing the movements of speech, or executing them with weakened muscles. Treated very differently from an articulation difficulty.

  • Childhood apraxia of speech — inconsistent errors, groping, worse with longer words
  • Dysarthria — weakness affecting clarity, volume, and breath support
  • High-frequency motor practice, cueing hierarchies, and prosody
  • AAC alongside, so the child isn't waiting years to be understood

What you'd see at homeThe same word said differently each time, visible searching for mouth position, far better understanding than production, speech that gets worse as sentences get longer.

Social communication

Using language with other people, and worth approaching as a two-way difference rather than a deficit to be corrected.

  • Conversation: starting, maintaining, ending, repairing
  • Taking another person's perspective and knowledge into account
  • Figurative language, sarcasm, indirect requests
  • Adjusting communication for different people and settings
  • Self-advocacy and describing your own needs

What you'd see at homeMonologues about a favorite topic, missing hints, taking things literally, difficulty entering a group, conversations that go fine one-to-one and not in a crowd.

Fluency and voice

How speech flows, and how it sounds. Both areas where the emotional impact often outruns the technical severity.

  • Stuttering — repetitions, prolongations, blocks, and the tension around them
  • Avoidance, word substitution, and reduced participation
  • Voice quality, loudness, pitch, and vocal strain
  • Resonance, including after palate surgery

What you'd see at homeGetting stuck on words, physical tension while speaking, avoiding phone calls and reading aloud, a hoarse voice that doesn't clear, a voice that's always too loud or too quiet.

Literacy and language for learning

An underused part of the scope. Reading and writing are language, and an SLP has a genuine role in both.

  • Phonological awareness — the foundation of decoding
  • Vocabulary and comprehension of academic text
  • Written narrative and organization
  • Literacy instruction for children who use AAC, including alphabet access

What you'd see at homeTrouble rhyming or hearing syllables, reading the words without getting the meaning, writing far below speaking ability.

Feeding and swallowing

With additional training, SLPs assess and treat the mechanics and safety of eating and drinking, often alongside occupational therapy.

  • Swallowing safety and aspiration risk
  • Chewing, texture management, and oral motor skill for eating
  • Bottle, cup, and straw drinking
  • Transition off tube feeding where appropriate
  • Coordination with instrumental swallow assessment

What you'd see at homeCoughing or wet breathing during meals, holding food in the cheeks, gagging on textures, very long meals, repeated chest infections.

Augmentative and alternative communication

Any way of communicating that supplements or replaces speech. For many children this is the central work rather than a fallback. See the full section below.

  • Speech-generating devices and communication apps
  • Low-tech boards and books, including partner-assisted approaches
  • Signs, gestures, and visual supports
  • Access methods: touch, keyguard, switch, eye gaze
  • Training the people around the child, which is most of the work

What you'd see at homeFrustration and escalation around being misunderstood, leading you by the hand, a device that arrived and is rarely used, communication that works at home and nowhere else.

Touch cues, and hands-on facilitation

A technique many families have never heard of, and one that a lot of speech therapists don't use, not because it's controversial, but because doing it well takes separate training that most have never had the chance to do.

Some people cannot get to a sound by hearing it or watching a mouth make it. The information they need is felt, where the jaw sits, how much the lips round, whether the tongue is up or back. Touch cues supply that information directly: the clinician places their hands on the jaw, cheeks, lips, chin, or under the chin to shape and stabilize the movement while the person is speaking.

It's most used with childhood apraxia of speech, dysarthria, and severe articulation difficulty — the situations where a person knows exactly what they want to say and cannot get their mouth to do it. The most established formal system is PROMPT, and tactile cueing also forms part of other motor speech approaches such as dynamic temporal and tactile cueing.

Why most therapists don't do it

Tactile cueing as a general idea is part of the standard set of cues every speech therapist learns — verbal, visual, gestural, tactile. What's uncommon is doing it systematically and skilfully, which is a genuine motor skill in the clinician's own hands and improves with training and practice.

Formal certification is a substantial commitment — multiple levels of coursework typically taken over several years, with practical requirements, which is simply out of reach for a lot of working clinicians. So it isn't that your therapist is behind; it's that this is a specialty within a specialty.

Who is most likely to have this in their hands

Motor speech specialists. Clinicians trained in PROMPT or in tactile-cueing motor speech approaches, and those whose caseload is mostly apraxia and dysarthria. This is the primary group. Ask directly whether a therapist is PROMPT-trained and at what level.

Orofacial myofunctional clinicians. Myofunctional training teaches tactile stimulation and oral placement techniques as a cueing system in their own right, and this work is hands-in-and-around-the-mouth by nature, so these clinicians tend to be comfortable and practiced with orofacial touch. The myofunctional therapy page covers that discipline in full.

If touch cues are something you want to try, those are the two places to look. A generalist speech therapist working mainly on language or phonology may never have needed the skill, and that is not a mark against them.

Before anyone puts hands on a person's face

  • Rapport comes first. Good practitioners spend sessions building a relationship before introducing touch at all. Anyone reaching for a face in the first ten minutes is doing it wrong.
  • Sensory tolerance is a real consideration, not an obstacle to be worked through. For a person with oral or facial sensitivity, this may need long desensitization, or may not be the right approach at all.
  • Assent matters, every single time. Ask, explain what you're about to do, and stop when the person indicates stop. Touch that is allowed to be refused is a completely different experience from touch that isn't.
  • It should fade. Touch cues are scaffolding. Ask what the plan is for reducing them, and by when.
  • Watch for distress, and say so. A person who becomes tense, avoidant, or upset about sessions is reporting something that outranks any protocol.

On the evidence: tactile cueing approaches have real support for motor speech disorders and are widely used, though reviews describe the research base as less robust than the popularity of these methods implies. Worth trying where it fits, and fair to ask the same question as anywhere else. What specifically should change, how will we measure it, and by when.

A different thing with the same name

"Touch cues" also means something else entirely in work with people who are deafblind or have combined sensory impairments: a small set of consistent touches on the body used to signal what is about to happen — a particular touch on the shoulder meaning I'm going to lift you, another meaning we're going to move.

That system is about predictability and consent rather than speech production, and it is enormously valuable for anyone who can't see or hear what is coming. If someone recommends touch cues, it's worth clarifying which of the two they mean.

AAC, properly

The most common reason AAC fails is not the wrong device. It is a device nobody was trained to use, holding a vocabulary too small to say anything interesting.

The four beliefs that cost children years

  • "AAC will stop them talking." The evidence points the other way — AAC use is associated with gains in spoken language, and this is one of the more consistent findings in the field. It is not a trade.
  • "They need to be ready first." There are no prerequisites for communication. Not matching pictures, not proving cause and effect, not a cognitive threshold, not good behavior. Access comes first, and understanding grows through use.
  • "It's a last resort." AAC belongs alongside speech work from the beginning, particularly in motor speech disorders where the gap between understanding and production is widest.
  • "They're too young." Toddlers use AAC. Waiting until school age wastes the years when language is most readily built.

What a real system contains

Vocabulary is usually described as core — the small set of high-frequency words that make up most of what anyone says, like go, more, stop, want, not, that, help, mine, and fringe, the specific nouns like dinosaur or trampoline. Systems heavy on fringe and light on core produce children who can name things and can't say anything about them.

And beyond both sits recounting — the ability to tell someone about something that already happened, which they did not witness. It needs past-tense marking, names of people and places, verbs for what people do, sequencing words, and feelings attached to a past event. Most systems never get there, and it is the vocabulary that lets a person report being hurt. Build it daily on ordinary happy events so the machinery exists before it is needed.

Check that these are present, and reachable in a couple of taps rather than buried:

The safety reason this matters

A system that can only make requests cannot report harm. A child who cannot say no, stop, that hurt, or name a body part or a person has no way to tell anyone that something has happened to them.

Ask your SLP to build refusal, protest, feelings, body, and people vocabulary explicitly. It is missing from many setups because nobody thought to ask for it, and it belongs in place long before it is ever needed.

How AAC actually gets learned

  • Aided language modeling. Adults use the system themselves, all day, while talking — pointing to words as they speak. Nobody becomes fluent in a language they have only ever been tested in.
  • Model declaratives, not just requests. Comment, notice, and wonder aloud on the device rather than only prompting for wants. This is what teaches that the system is for saying things, not only for getting things. See the behavior page for how declarative language works and why it matters.
  • No demands attached. Model far more than you prompt. A device used only when an adult wants an answer becomes an unpleasant object.
  • Motor planning consistency. Words should stay in the same place. Rearranging a display resets the physical memory a child has built, so ask before anyone reorganizes it.
  • Present everywhere, always charged. Specials, lunch, playground, bus, bath, car, hospital. A device left in a backpack is a device that doesn't exist.
  • Everyone trained. Not just the SLP — teachers, aides, grandparents, babysitters, bus drivers. Written into the IEP with a named person and a frequency.
  • Never taken away as a consequence. Removing a device is removing a voice.
  • Assume competence and give response time. Composing a message takes far longer than speaking one. Wait longer than feels comfortable.

Choosing a system

A good evaluation is a trial rather than a recommendation on paper. It compares systems with your actual child, considering vocabulary organization, access method, physical positioning, vision needs, durability, voice options, and — critically — whether the system can grow for the next decade rather than the next year. The written report is what supports insurance funding, so ask that it be built for that purpose.

On methods involving physical support

Families researching AAC will encounter approaches in which a facilitator provides physical support to a person's hand, wrist, or arm, or holds the letterboard. Major professional associations in speech pathology, psychology, and intellectual disability have taken formal positions against facilitated communication, and have raised serious concerns about related prompting methods, on the grounds that the message may originate with the facilitator rather than the person.

This matters more here than anywhere else on this site, because the entire purpose of AAC is that the words are the person's. The protective standard is independent, verifiable authorship — a person selecting messages without physical contact, and able to convey information the facilitator does not know. Any method should be moving toward that, and be willing to demonstrate it.

This is a painful area for many families, and the desire behind it is entirely understandable. That is exactly why the standard matters: a person's voice is only theirs if it can be shown to be theirs.

Why a device, and not only a board

The most common question families are asked to justify, and the one the professions have already answered.

The goal is independent access to a dynamic, speech-generating communication device. One the person operates themselves, that speaks out loud, and that holds far more language than any single board can.

Low-tech boards are useful. They are also, on their own, not enough, and it is worth being precise about why.

What pointing at a board cannot do

  • It requires somebody to be watching. A person pointing at a card in an empty room has said nothing. A device speaks whether or not anyone was looking.
  • It requires somebody to interpret correctly. The message passes through another person's reading of it, and if they misread, guess, or fill in what they expect, the person has been spoken for rather than heard.
  • It cannot reach across a room. No calling out, no joining in from the doorway, no interrupting, no being noticed by someone who was not already attending.
  • Only strangers-who-know-them can understand it. A speech output device is understood by a shopkeeper, a substitute teacher, a lifeguard, a paramedic. A laminated board mostly is not.
  • It holds thirty words. A robust device holds thousands, arranged so novel sentences can be built, including things nobody anticipated they would want to say.
  • It cannot say the unexpected. And the unexpected is most of what a person needs to tell you, particularly if something is wrong.

Being understood only by the people who already know you well is not the same as being heard.

Where low-tech belongs

Not as a substitute, and not as a stage somebody must pass before earning a device. As support alongside one:

  • When the device cannot be there — the pool, the bath, the beach, a rough-and-tumble activity.
  • When it is charging, broken, or away for repair. Every person using a device needs a backup that requires no battery. This is the most common reason and the most often overlooked.
  • As an activity-specific extra beside the device — a board at the table does not replace the device on the table, it sits next to it.
  • While learning, and while a family or school builds fluency, but running in parallel with device access, not before it.
  • For a partner to model on, so an adult can point while talking without taking the person's device away from them.

This is a professional standard, not just a value

The Communication Bill of Rights, from the National Joint Committee for the Communicative Needs of Persons with Severe Disabilities, and endorsed by ASHA among others — sets out that every person communicates, and holds rights including the right to request, to refuse, to be spoken to with dignity, to be included in conversations about them, and to have access to functioning communication supports at all times.

Alongside it, the Committee's position on eligibility explicitly rejects what is known as the candidacy model. No cognitive test score, no demonstrated prerequisite skill, no proof of readiness may be required before a person is given access to communication supports. There is no threshold to clear.

Which means a family asking for communication access is not asking for a favor or an unusual accommodation. They are asking for something the professions have already agreed is a right, and which is, in practice, denied to people constantly.

Things that are said, and are not true

  • "He needs to show us he is ready." Readiness criteria are precisely what the candidacy model is, and precisely what the professional position rejects.
  • "A device will stop him talking." The evidence runs the other way. Providing communication support either increases speech or makes no difference; it does not suppress it.
  • "He can already point, so he does not need one." Pointing depends on an audience and an interpreter. That is not the same thing.
  • "Let's start with a few pictures and see how he does." Reasonable as a first week. Not reasonable as a destination, and not a reason to delay a device.
  • "He only uses it to request snacks." Then the vocabulary is too small, or it is only ever offered at snack time. Both are fixable and neither is about the person.
  • "It is too expensive." Devices are often funded through insurance or Medicaid as durable medical equipment with a speech therapist's evaluation, and through school systems where communication is educationally necessary. See paying for it and inside the IEP.

If you are being told your person is not ready, the phrase worth using is: "Which professional standard is that based on? My understanding is that eligibility criteria for communication access were rejected by the field." Then ask for the answer in writing.

Making it part of the day

The difference between a device that transforms a life and one that lives in a bag is almost never the device. It is whether the adults around it changed how they talk.

The single practice that matters most

Model it constantly, and expect nothing back. Use the device or board yourself while you talk — several times a meal, all day, for months — without asking the person to respond, without waiting expectantly, without turning it into a question.

Children hear tens of thousands of spoken words before saying one. A person learning aided language deserves the same immersion, and almost never gets it. If nobody models, the person is being asked to learn a language they have never heard anyone speak.

At home, in ordinary moments

The goal is that using it stops being an event.

  • It is available every waking hour, in every room, including the car
  • Model during things that are already happening — cooking, television, the walk to the car
  • Comment far more than you request or question
  • Respond to every use, including the ones you did not want to hear
  • Siblings and grandparents use it too, out loud, for fun
  • Leave silence after modeling — count to ten before filling it

The testCould they tell you something you were not expecting, right now, without anybody fetching anything?

In therapy sessions

What good looks like, from any discipline.

  • The device is present in every session, not only speech
  • The therapist models on it themselves, fluently, having learned the layout
  • Vocabulary is taught inside real activities, not drilled in isolation
  • Targets cover commenting, refusing, asking and joking, not requesting alone
  • The person is never required to use it to earn something they already have a right to
  • Goals travel home. See the goals tracker

Fair to ask"Can you show me you using it?" A therapist who cannot navigate it is not yet able to teach it.

At school

Where access most often quietly disappears.

  • Access written into the IEP across all settings, including lunch, recess, specials, the bus and fire drills
  • Named staff responsible for charging it, daily
  • Every adult who works with the person trained to model, not only the SLP
  • Never removed as a consequence, and never left at the front of the room
  • Vocabulary added for what is actually happening that term
  • A backup that needs no battery, kept in the classroom

The common failureIt comes home each night uncharged and unused. That is not a device problem, and it is entirely addressable.

Building fluency, honestly

What the first year tends to look like.

  • Months of modeling before much comes back is normal, not failure
  • Early use is often requesting. That is a starting point, not a ceiling
  • Exploration and apparent nonsense are how a language gets learned
  • Plateaus happen; they usually mean the vocabulary needs to grow, not shrink
  • Do not reduce a device because it looks like too many buttons. That removes the words nobody knew they needed

Worth rememberingNobody takes words away from a speaking child for using them wrongly.

When it is not working

Questions in the order worth asking them.

  • Is it physically available all day, charged, and within reach?
  • Is anybody modeling on it — honestly, how often?
  • Is it only ever offered at demand moments?
  • Can it say no, stop, and I don't like this, and is that honoured?
  • Is access physically easy, or is motor difficulty being read as unwillingness?
  • Has the vocabulary grown in the last six months?

UsuallyThe answer is in the first two questions.

Three things that should never happen

Never take it away. Not as a consequence, not to encourage speech, not because it is being used to say something inconvenient. Removing somebody's device is removing their voice, and it is not a behavior strategy.

Never require it to earn something. Making a person request what they are entitled to teaches that communication is a toll rather than a right.

Never ignore a message you did not want. A refusal that is overridden teaches, in one moment, that the whole system is decorative.

Teaching someone to recount

Telling a person who wasn't there about something that already happened. It's the vocabulary that carries a life between settings and the vocabulary that lets someone report being hurt, and it is the one most systems never reach.

Most communication work stays in the here and now: requesting what's in the room, commenting on what's in front of them. Recounting is a different task and it has to be built deliberately. It needs past-tense marking, names of people and places, verbs for what people do, sequencing words, and feelings attached to a past event.

The behavior page sets out why this matters so much for safety. What follows is how to teach it.

Practice on the good things

Here is the part that makes it work: you build recounting on ordinary and happy events, every day, so that the machinery already exists when something bad happens. Nobody can learn to narrate a frightening experience during the frightening experience.

So ask about the swimming lesson. Ask who was there. Ask what happened first. Model it constantly — tell them about your own day, out loud, in the same structure, with the same words. Send a photo home from school so there's a shared reference to talk from. Use their device for it, not just for requests, so recounting is a thing that page does.

A person who has recounted a thousand small good things has the words, the format, and — crucially — the expectation of being listened to. That expectation is what makes a disclosure possible at all.

Start much earlier than open questions

"Tell me about today" is an enormous ask. It requires choosing an event from a whole day, retrieving it, sequencing it, and producing it from nothing. For most people building this skill, that is several years ahead of where they are, and asking it too early teaches only that the question has no answer.

Build it the other way round, starting with a closed frame the adult fills in.

The three-column frame

A dedicated page or low-tech board with three columns — who, what they did, and what they did it to. Add a past-tense marker they can press, and later a column for the people in their life.

Whomommypeople and names
Did whatplay+ ed for past
Whatblocksthings and places
Added laterwithjoining word
Who elseJanetheir people

"Mommy played blocks, with Jane."

Color-code the columns, and match whatever their device already uses. Most systems follow a widely used convention where yellow marks people and pronouns, green marks actions, orange marks things, and white or gray marks the small joining words. Consistency with their system matters far more than matching any standard — if their device colors things differently, copy their device.

The colors are doing real work. They let someone find the right kind of word by location and color rather than by reading, they make the sentence structure visible, and they mean a low-tech board and a tablet page can be used interchangeably without relearning anything.

The frame does the hard part. Instead of generating a sentence, the person is choosing between visible options, which is a completely different cognitive task and a reachable one. The past-tense button matters more than it looks: it is the thing that marks this already happened, which is the whole foundation of reporting.

Build it low-tech as well as on the tablet

Whatever system someone uses, they need a paper version of the same thing, not as a lesser substitute, but because devices run out of battery, go for repair, get left at school, and cannot come in the pool or the bath. Every device user should have a low-tech backup, and the backup should look like the device.

Making one

  • The board. A cotton-covered cardboard painting board — the cheap artist's canvas boards from any craft shop — works beautifully. The fabric surface grips hook velcro without any adhesive backing failing, it's rigid enough to hold on a lap, and it's light enough to carry.
  • The dots. Adhesive velcro dots on the board in a grid, and the opposite side of the velcro on the back of each card. Cards come off, go on, and swap out as vocabulary grows.
  • The cards. Print, laminate, and cut. Laminating matters more than it sounds — these get handled constantly, taken outdoors, and dropped in food.
  • The colors. Print each card on its color-coded background, matching the tablet page exactly. Same colors, same positions, same layout.
  • Storage. An envelope taped to the back, or a ring of extra cards clipped to a corner, so vocabulary that isn't on the board today is still within reach.
  • Make more than one. One at home, one in the bag, one at school. They get lost, and a lost board should be a nuisance rather than a crisis.

Use real photographs wherever you can

On both the board and the tablet page, customized images beat generic symbols nearly every time. Not a clip-art drawing of a slide — their slide, at their playground. Not a stock figure labeled "teacher" — a photograph of the actual person, with their actual name under it.

Recognition is faster, the meaning is unambiguous, and it turns the system into a record of their real life rather than a set of abstractions. It also makes recounting possible: a person can point to the actual human being who was there.

Some practical notes. Take photos deliberately, of staff, classrooms, vehicles, the bathroom, the therapy room, the people in their week, and ask school to send some too; most will if you explain why. Shoot on plain uncluttered backgrounds with the subject filling the frame, which matters enormously for anyone with cerebral visual impairment. Keep a folder so replacing a worn card takes two minutes. And update them as people and places change, because an out-of-date board quietly teaches that the system doesn't match reality.

The progression

Model it immediately after the activity

The adult builds the sentence on the board or device while the memory is fresh and the objects are still in sight. No question is asked and nothing is required of the person. Do this many times a day.

Model it, then ask yes or no

Build the sentence, then check it. A yes-or-no answer is achievable long before a narrative is. "You climbed the slide with Jane. Is that right?"

Leave one slot for them

Fill in two columns and pause at the third. Then swap which column you leave open, so they aren't only ever choosing the object. "You climbed the ___ with Jane."

They build it with the frame in front of them

The columns are still there, doing the structural work. They are now selecting across all of it, with the adult only prompting where it stalls.

They recount with a cue, not a frame

A photo, an object from the day, a line in the home log. Something concrete to talk from, but the sentence is theirs.

Open questions

Only now. "Tell me about today." "Who was there?" "What happened then?", and wait far longer than is comfortable.

The home log as the bridge

This is what makes it work across settings, and Ask school for specifically. Have the daily log record the activities themselves, in frame-shaped detail — who, what, with whom, not just had a good day.

Then at home, read the log first, and model it back on the device:

"I heard that at school today. You climbed the slide with Jane. Is that right?"

Now something remarkable is available. The adult knows enough to ask a real question about a real event the adult was not present for, and the person gets the experience of being asked about their own life by someone who is interested. Congratulate the thing they did. Ask whether it was scary. Talk about the bit where they slipped getting off, and how that felt.

That exchange is not a language exercise. It is a person telling someone who loves them about their day and being met, which is what relationships are made of, and what a great many people with communication differences have never once had. The reporting skill this builds is essential and it is the byproduct. The connection is the point.

Approaches, sorted by how well they're supported

Well supported

AAC introduction alongside speech work, including for very young children.

Parent-implemented language intervention and naturalistic approaches embedded in play and routines — among the strongest findings for early language.

Focused stimulation and enhanced milieu teaching for early vocabulary and grammar.

Phonological awareness instruction as a route into reading.

Established speech sound approaches — minimal pairs, complexity approaches, traditional articulation therapy.

Motor-based treatment for childhood apraxia, delivered frequently and in short sessions.

Early stuttering intervention in preschool.

Video modeling for specific social communication targets.

Telepractice — comparable outcomes to in-person for many goals.

Mixed or emerging

Gestalt language processing and natural language acquisition frameworks — increasingly popular, clinically compelling to many families and therapists, and still supported mainly by descriptive rather than controlled research. Worth using thoughtfully and worth knowing the evidence is young.

Social skills groups — modest gains, with generalization outside the group being the persistent problem.

Tactile-kinesthetic cueing approaches for motor speech. Some support, less than their popularity implies.

Packaged social cognition curricula — widely used, mixed evidence, and criticized by some adults with autism for teaching masking rather than genuine communication.

Little support. Ask hard questions

What SLPs work on at each stage

Birth to three

Foundations, through the people already there

Early gestures, joint attention, first words, feeding, and coaching families to build language into everyday routines. AAC belongs here too, not later. Referral is worth making on a suspicion rather than a certainty.

Preschool

Vocabulary, sentences, and being understood

Vocabulary explosion, sentence building, speech sound development, early social communication, and early literacy foundations. This is also when a robust AAC system should be fully in place if speech isn't yet reliable.

Elementary

Language for learning

Following classroom instruction, narrative, reading comprehension, written expression, conversation with peers, and carryover of speech sounds into everyday talk. AAC vocabulary expands into academic and social language.

Middle and high school

Complexity, and self-knowledge

Figurative and academic language, group conversation, advocating for accommodations, understanding their own communication profile, and, for AAC users — speed, independence, and literacy for independent message construction.

Transition to adulthood

Communication in the world they're entering

Workplace and college communication, disclosure decisions, managing their own device and its funding, and knowing how to explain to a stranger how to communicate with them.

Recognizing strong speech therapy

What good looks like

  • Goals you can picture happening in your kitchen, not just in a session
  • Comprehension and expression assessed separately, and the gap named
  • AAC raised early, without being framed as giving up
  • Parents coached, not just updated at pickup
  • Progress measured and shown, including carryover outside the room
  • Communication treated as the goal rather than speech specifically
  • The child's own attempts honored, in whatever form they arrive
  • Honest handoffs when another discipline should lead

Worth asking about

  • The same worksheet or flashcard drill for a year with no carryover plan
  • Non-speech oral motor exercises presented as speech treatment
  • AAC deferred until speech has been "given a chance"
  • A device introduced with no plan to train the adults around the child
  • Goals about compliance — sitting, waiting, quiet hands — rather than communication
  • Echolalia treated as something to eliminate rather than understand
  • Parents kept out of sessions as policy
  • Progress described only as "she's doing great"

Questions ask

  • What's the gap between what my child understands and what they can say?
  • What are we doing right now, today, so they can be understood while we work on speech?
  • Which communication functions can they currently use, and which are missing?
  • How do you train me, and the school, and everyone else?
  • What's your experience with AAC, and with children like mine?
  • How do you know progress is real and not just better test-taking?
  • What would make you say we're finished here?

School SLP and private SLP

School speech therapy exists to support educational access, is often delivered in small groups, and is measured in minutes on an IEP. Ask whether they're direct or consultative. Private therapy can pursue anything you and the therapist agree matters, including social communication, feeding, and AAC implementation at home. Many families use both; ask the two therapists to coordinate so goals don't duplicate.

Frequency

Motor speech disorders generally need frequent, short sessions. This is one of the clearer dosage findings. Language goals often do better with less frequent sessions plus strong parent coaching and daily practice embedded in routines. Ask which model applies to your child and why.

Paying for it

  • Verify coverage by code and place of service before starting. Ask whether evaluations count against annual visit limits.
  • AAC devices are usually funded separately as durable medical equipment and require a formal SLP evaluation with specific documentation language. Ask the SLP to write it for funding from the outset.
  • School-provided devices may not come home or travel with a child between districts. Ask, in writing, before you rely on one.

Before you close this

The whole page in one line: the goal was never speech. It was being understood, and being able to say the things that matter.

If you take one action from here, make it this — look at whatever system your child has right now, and check whether they can refuse, ask a question, and tell someone something hurts. If any of those are missing, that is the next conversation, and it is a conversation you are fully equipped to start.

This is a map, not medical advice. Assessment and treatment decisions belong to clinicians who have met your child. Scope of practice, licensure, and school service rules vary by state.

Where evidence for a specific approach is limited or contested, this page says so. That isn't a reason to avoid it — it's a reason to ask what outcome is expected, how it will be measured, and by when.
