Supplements and natural approaches:
what to ask about

A reference list of what families commonly try and what the evidence actually says — organized so you can take questions to the people treating your child. It is not a recommendation of anything, and it deliberately contains no doses.

Read this part first

This page will not tell you what to give anybody. There are no amounts here on purpose. Dosing depends on weight, age, kidney and liver function, existing medication and what is actually wrong, and getting it from a website is how children end up harmed.

What it is for: so that when something comes up, in an article, a video, a post shared in a group, from another parent, at a clinic, or on a shelf in a shop. You have some foundation under you. What the thing actually is. What it is claimed to do. Whether that claim holds up. And what to ask your prescribing doctor about it.

Anything on this page belongs in a conversation with a licensed medical doctor — the same one who prescribes. Supplements are not a separate category from medication. They enter the same body, use the same liver and kidneys, and interact with the same prescriptions, so the person deciding about one needs to know about the other.

This page exists because families use these things whether or not anybody discusses them, and because the alternative source of information is usually somebody selling the product, or an algorithm that has noticed you are worried about your child.

Five things before anything else

Some difficulties do have a treatable cause underneath. See when something underneath is treatable. This page is about the specific things families try, and what the evidence says.

These matter more than any individual item further down.

  • Tell your prescribing doctor everything your child takes, including supplements. The licensed medical doctor who writes the prescriptions, not only the practitioner who suggested the supplement. Bring the actual bottles or a list. Roughly half of families do not mention supplements to their doctor, often because they expect dismissal, and that silence is where the real danger sits, because interactions cannot be checked against something nobody knows about. The medication list holds supplements alongside prescriptions for exactly this reason.
  • "Natural" does not mean safe, gentle or inert. Plants make powerful chemicals; several common medications are derived from them. Something strong enough to help is strong enough to interact, and herbs in particular can amplify or block prescription medication.
  • Supplements are not regulated like medicine. In the US they do not require proof of effectiveness or safety before sale, and contamination is a real and documented problem, including heavy metals in botanical products. Judging what you are offered covers how to check.
  • Test before you treat, where a test exists. Iron, vitamin D, B12, zinc, thyroid and folate can all be measured. Treating a deficiency somebody does not have is at best pointless and at worst harmful, and several of these are genuinely dangerous in excess.
  • One thing at a time, with a decision point. Start one, wait a defined period, and decide. Families often end up giving fifteen things, unable to say what any of them did, at considerable expense. Write down what you are watching for before you start — the symptom tracker keeps that record.

And rule out the ordinary explanation first

Poor sleep can be an airway problem. Irritability can be pain, constipation, or hunger. Fatigue can be iron or thyroid. Difficulty concentrating can be sleep.

Supplements are frequently reached for at the point where the actual cause has not been looked for. The whole picture covers the order that finds treatable things first.

Things that can be measured

The strongest ground on this page. Where a deficiency is real and documented, correcting it is medicine rather than guesswork, and the improvement can be substantial.

Iron

Deficiency is common in restricted eaters and often missed because standard blood counts can look normal while stores are low.

  • Ask for ferritin, not just hemoglobin
  • Low iron is associated with fatigue, poor concentration, restless legs and disturbed sleep
  • Excess iron is toxic. This is one to test rather than assume

Note"Could we check ferritin?" is among the highest-value questions on this page.

Vitamin D

Low levels are widespread, more so with limited outdoor time, darker skin, or restricted diets.

  • Testable, and correctable
  • Very high doses over long periods cause harm
  • Often bundled into other products — check you are not doubling

What this meansWhether it has ever been measured, and when.

Vitamin B12 and folate

Relevant with restricted diets, gut conditions, vegan diets, and some medications.

  • Both measurable
  • Deficiency can affect mood, energy and nerve function
  • Some conditions affect how folate is transported into the brain, which is a specific and testable question rather than a general one

Worth askingIf a clinician raises folate transport, ask what test confirms it and what result would change the plan.

Zinc, magnesium, thyroid

All measurable, all occasionally relevant, all frequently supplemented without ever being checked.

  • Zinc affects taste and appetite; deficiency is plausible with very restricted eating
  • Magnesium blood levels are an imperfect measure, which is good to know
  • Thyroid is not a supplement question but belongs on any fatigue or growth workup

What mattersWhat was tested, when, and what the numbers actually were, not just "it was normal".

What families most often ask about

Grouped by what people are hoping for, with the honest position on each. None of this is a recommendation.

For sleep

  • Melatonin — the most studied supplement in this whole area, with reasonable evidence for helping people fall asleep, particularly with neurodevelopmental conditions. It helps sleep onset more than staying asleep. Timing matters more than amount, and more is not better. Discuss it with your prescribing doctor; it is a hormone, not a vitamin, and long-term effects in children are still being studied. Accidental overdose in children has risen sharply — store it like medication, not like sweets.
  • Iron, where ferritin is low and restless legs are in the picture.
  • Magnesium — widely used, thin evidence for sleep specifically. Some forms loosen the bowels, which is sometimes the actual reason it seems to help.
  • Airway first. If there is snoring, mouth breathing or restless sleep, no supplement addresses that. See myofunctional therapy.

For calm, and for anxiety

A large category, sold heavily, and the one where marketing language runs furthest ahead of the evidence.

  • L-theanine — an amino acid found in tea. Among the better-supported things here, with modest evidence for a calmer state without sedation, and generally well tolerated. Modest is the operative word.
  • GABA — sold widely on the reasoning that it is the brain's calming neurotransmitter, so taking it should calm you. The difficulty is that GABA taken by mouth does not readily cross into the brain. Products using liposomal or other delivery systems claim to solve this; whether they actually do is claimed more often than it is demonstrated. If something containing it seems to help, that is worth noticing, but the mechanism on the label may not be the reason.
  • Passionflower — a traditional calming herb with some small trials for anxiety in adults, and much less evidence in children. Generally well tolerated. It is sedating, which is the next point.
  • Sedatives stack. Passionflower, valerian, chamomile, melatonin, magnesium and prescription sleep or anxiety medication all pull in the same direction, and a child taking several may be much more sedated than anyone intended. List them all for the prescribing doctor together, not one at a time.
  • "Liposomal" is a delivery claim, not a quality grade. Genuine liposomal preparation can improve absorption of some substances; the word on a label does not by itself tell you the product does. It usually costs considerably more, which is worth weighing against evidence for that specific product.
  • What has better evidence than any of these for childhood anxiety is cognitive behavioral therapy, and for OCD specifically, exposure and response prevention. See anxiety and OCD. That is not a reason to avoid a supplement; it is a reason not to let one substitute for the thing that works.

For attention and regulation

  • Omega-3 and DHA — extensively studied, small effects at best, generally safe. Covered more fully below.
  • Iron and zinc, where measured and low.
  • Elimination diets and food dye removal — a minority of children respond to specific dye removal. Broad elimination diets are not supported and carry real nutritional and social costs, particularly for someone already eating a narrow range.
  • Caffeine, nicotine analogues, and "focus" blends — avoid in children unless a prescribing doctor has specifically advised it.

Omega-3 and DHA

  • Among the most studied supplements here, and the honest summary is that effects on attention and behavior are small. Real in some analyses, modest, and far less than the marketing implies.
  • DHA specifically matters for brain and eye development, which is why it appears in infant formula. That is not the same as evidence that adding more helps an older child who is already getting enough.
  • Reasonable to try, unreasonable to expect much, and it is one of the safer things on this page, which is part of why it is so widely recommended.
  • Practical points: it can cause fishy burps and stomach upset, which sinks compliance quickly; oils oxidize, so freshness and storage matter; algae-derived versions exist for anyone avoiding fish; and it has a mild blood-thinning effect worth mentioning before surgery or alongside anticoagulants.
  • Fish allergy is an obvious but often missed consideration.

For energy, muscle tone and mitochondrial support

A cluster of products sold together, often to families whose child has low tone, tires easily, or has been told something about energy metabolism.

  • Low muscle tone is a finding, not a diagnosis. It has many causes. Some genetic, some neurological, some connective tissue, many never fully explained, and the question fair to ask is what is causing it, not what to add. The treatment with actual evidence is physical therapy and activity, not a supplement. See physical therapy.
  • Mitochondrial cocktails — combinations of CoQ10, carnitine, riboflavin, alpha-lipoic acid and creatine are used in diagnosed primary mitochondrial disease. Even there the evidence is modest and specialists differ on which components help. Beyond that diagnosis, this is extrapolation from a mechanism rather than a treatment with results behind it.
  • CoQ10. There are genuine deficiency states, and it is reasonably well tolerated. It interacts with warfarin, and absorption varies a great deal between preparations. Widely sold far beyond where the evidence sits.
  • Supplemental ATP — the reasoning sounds compelling: cells run on ATP, so supply more. In practice ATP taken by mouth is broken down in digestion rather than delivered to cells, and the premise does not survive contact with how the body actually works. Be cautious with anything sold on that logic.
  • Glutathione — the body's main internal antioxidant, and heavily marketed on that basis. Taken by mouth it is largely broken down in digestion, which is why liposomal and intravenous versions are sold instead; absorption from those varies and the clinical evidence remains thin. NAC is a precursor the body uses to make its own and is better absorbed, which is one reason it appears more often in research. Intravenous administration in children outside a hospital setting is not a small thing and deserves a second opinion.
  • Carnitine and creatine. Both have specific, testable deficiency states where treatment is indicated. Both are also sold generally on much thinner grounds.
  • If mitochondrial disease is suspected, that is a referral to a metabolic or neurometabolic specialist, not a supplement decision. The workup is specific, and a real diagnosis changes anesthesia planning, illness management and much else, which is worth having properly established either way.

For constipation

Worth more space than the rest, because it is common, frequently undertreated, and one of the most reliable hidden causes of pain, appetite loss and difficult behavior.

  • Fiber and fluid first — unglamorous, and the place to start.
  • Two different kinds of laxative, often confused. Osmotic ones draw water into the stool and are generally what guidelines favor first for children, including for long-term maintenance. Stimulant ones — senna, and bisacodyl — make the bowel contract, and are usually added rather than started with.
  • Senna, including the gummy versions, is a stimulant. It is widely used and often helpful, and it is a medication rather than a food. Ask the prescribing doctor whether it suits this child, alongside what, and for how long.
  • One specific caution worth knowing about senna: prolonged skin contact with senna-containing stool has caused significant chemical burns and blistering in young children, particularly in diapers or where a change is delayed. If it is being used with a child who is not toileting independently, change promptly and tell the prescribing doctor about any unexplained redness or blistering.
  • The most common failure is undertreatment, not overtreatment. Chronic constipation usually needs a clear-out first and then months of maintenance, and families very often stop as soon as things improve, which is precisely when it returns. Ask what the plan is, in weeks, and what stopping looks like.
  • Aloe is worth a specific note. Aloe latex — the bitter layer just under the leaf skin — is a stimulant laxative in the same family as senna, and it was removed from over-the-counter laxative products in the US because safety data was lacking. Aloe gel or juice is a different part of the plant and a different thing, though products are often unclear about which they contain. For a child, this is one to ask the prescribing doctor about rather than pick off a shelf, and there are better-studied options for the same job.
  • Withholding is not the same as constipation, though each causes the other. A child who has had one painful experience may hold on for months afterward, and that fear needs addressing alongside the physical side.

Probiotics

  • Effects are strain-specific, not general. Evidence for one strain says nothing about another, and most products do not tell you which they contain in a way you can check against research.
  • Best evidence is for particular situations — antibiotic-associated diarrhea, some infant colic, certain gut conditions.
  • Much weaker for behavior, mood, sleep or autism symptoms, despite marketing that leans heavily in that direction.
  • Not a treatment for constipation. Frequently reached for instead of treating it, which delays the thing that would actually help.
  • Spore-based products are a different category, and the difference is real. They use bacillus species that form protective spores, which survive stomach acid and do not need refrigeration. That shelf-stability is the genuine advantage, and it is also why they work in a gummy where a conventional probiotic largely would not survive processing. It is one of the more technically coherent gummy products on a shelf.
  • Surviving the journey is not the same as doing something useful once it arrives. Particular bacillus strains have evidence for particular situations; the broader claims about reseeding or restoring a microbiome go well beyond what has been shown, for spore-based products as much as any other kind.
  • The strain question still applies. Ask which strains, and what evidence exists for those specific ones in the situation you are treating, not for the category.
  • A real caution: children who are immunocompromised, seriously unwell, or have a central line should not take probiotics of any kind without specialist agreement. There are documented cases of serious infection.

Other gut questions

  • Digestive enzymes — essential for specific diagnosed conditions such as pancreatic insufficiency, not supported as a general intervention.
  • Reflux — a medical question with real treatments, and worth investigating rather than managing with supplements.
  • Digestive bitters — traditional preparations taken before meals, working by stimulating saliva and stomach secretions. Low risk for most people and very little formal evidence either way. Two practical points for a child: most are alcohol-based tinctures, and some contain wormwood or gentian, which are not neutral ingredients. Check what is in it and mention it to the prescribing doctor.
  • Bovine immunoglobulin products — sold over the counter for gut and immune support, and also available as a prescription medical food. The prescription version has evidence in specific diagnosed conditions; the over-the-counter versions are marketed far more broadly than that evidence reaches, often using the language of intestinal permeability. Derived from cows, so it matters if dairy or beef is an issue. Worth asking what specifically it is being used for and what would show whether it worked.

For frequent illness

  • Vitamin D, where low.
  • Zinc. Some evidence for shortening colds; long-term high-dose use interferes with copper.
  • Elderberry, echinacea, vitamin C — popular, mild evidence at best, generally low risk.
  • Recurrent infection is a workup question, not a supplement question. Repeated ear infections, pneumonias or unusual infections deserve investigation.

Multivitamins

  • Reasonable insurance for a restricted diet, and one of the more sensible things on this page for a child eating a very short list of foods. Discuss it with the prescribing doctor or a dietitian rather than choosing by packaging.
  • Not a substitute for finding out what is low. A general multivitamin will not correct a real iron or vitamin D deficiency, and may give false reassurance that it has.
  • Check for duplication. Once several products are in use, the same nutrient often appears in three of them. That is how children reach unsafe totals of vitamin A, D or iron without anyone intending it.
  • Be wary of high-dose formulas marketed for specific conditions. Several sold for neurodevelopmental conditions contain amounts well above established upper limits for children.
  • Ask a dietitian if you can. Underused, often available through a hospital or clinic, and far better placed than a shop assistant to say what is actually missing.

A word about gummies specifically

Gummy versions of almost everything now exist, and they solve a real problem, for a child who cannot swallow pills, a gummy may be the only workable form. That is a legitimate reason to use them.

But the format brings its own issues, and they are rarely mentioned on the packet:

  • Children treat them as sweets, because they are indistinguishable from sweets. Accidental overdose is common enough that poison centers track it. Store them exactly as you would store medication — out of reach, and not in the kitchen cupboard with the snacks.
  • They stick to teeth. Sugar and acidity, held against the enamel, in children who often already find brushing difficult.
  • Gummy multivitamins usually contain no iron, which is one of the more likely things to actually be low.
  • The amount per gummy varies enormously between brands, so a dose worked out for one product does not transfer to another.

None of this means avoid them. It means treat them as medication in a sweet-shaped package, which is exactly what they are.

Two that are asked about constantly, and are genuinely mixed

Folinic acid

A form of folate that crosses into the brain by a different route from ordinary folic acid. Asked about most often in relation to speech and language.

  • There is a real, diagnosable condition here. Cerebral folate deficiency means folate is not reaching the brain properly despite normal blood levels. It is identified by specific testing, and where it is confirmed, treatment is indicated rather than speculative.
  • Beyond that group the picture is contested. Several small trials have looked at high-dose folinic acid for verbal communication in autism with mixed and not consistently replicated results. It is an area of active research rather than a settled treatment.
  • The antibody blood test used to justify it is disputed. It is a proxy for a question the definitive test answers directly, and clinicians differ on how much weight a positive carries. A practitioner should be able to say plainly which view they hold, what the definitive test would be, and why they are using this one instead. See judging what you are offered below.
  • A lot of it is extrapolated from genetic reports. A common gene variant is frequently presented as requiring supplementation. That reasoning goes much beyond what the variant is known to mean.
  • There are three different things sharing the name, and the distinction matters. An over-the-counter supplement, unregulated and generally at modest amounts. A standard prescription — the same molecule, an established drug with approved uses, made to pharmaceutical standards. And high-dose prescribing, which is that same drug at considerably higher amounts than its approved uses, given off-label. The first two are not the third, and most of the autism research is about the third.
  • Higher doses bring more reported effects — irritability, overactivity and disturbed sleep are the ones families most often describe, and they can be mistaken for the condition worsening rather than a response to the treatment. Worth agreeing in advance how you would tell them apart.
  • One genuine safety point: B12 status should be established before anyone starts substantial folate supplementation. High folate can correct the blood picture of B12 deficiency while nerve damage continues underneath, which is exactly the kind of harm that happens quietly.

What to askWhat specific finding justifies this, what improvement would look like, by when, and what result would make you stop.

NAC, and picking or pulling

N-acetylcysteine is asked about most often for nail biting, skin picking and hair pulling, which together have a name most families are never given: body-focused repetitive behaviors. The conditions library has a fuller entry.

  • Knowing the category helps more than the supplement does. These behaviors are common, they are not defiance, and they are usually self-regulatory rather than deliberate. They tend to increase with stress, boredom and understimulation.
  • The best-evidenced treatment is behavioral, not a supplement. Habit reversal training — noticing the early cue and substituting a competing action — has the strongest support, and is often available through a psychologist or an OT.
  • NAC itself has genuinely mixed evidence. A well-known adult trial in hair pulling found benefit; a children's trial did not. Results in autism have been mixed. It is not nothing, and it is not established.
  • It is slow. Where it helps at all, it takes weeks rather than days, which matters when deciding how long to give it.
  • Check it with the prescribing doctor. It can upset the stomach, and there are specific cautions with asthma and with certain heart medications.

Rule out firstSkin conditions, eczema, dry skin and hangnails all drive picking, and treating the skin sometimes ends it. Sudden onset or worsening is worth a look at what changed.

Turmeric, and botanicals used in protocols

  • Turmeric and curcumin are asked about constantly as a general anti-inflammatory. Human evidence is modest and inconsistent — the most reasonable showing is in osteoarthritis, and it is not dramatic. Curcumin is also poorly absorbed on its own, which is why products add black pepper extract to boost it, and that same compound increases absorption of other things, including medications, which is a real interaction mechanism rather than a theoretical one.
  • Two cautions on turmeric specifically. It has been a repeated source of lead contamination, in some supply chains a lead compound has been added to brighten the color, and lead is the last thing you want in a child. And turmeric products have become one of the more often reported causes of supplement-associated liver injury. Neither means avoid it; both mean buy carefully and mention it to the prescribing doctor.
  • Cryptolepis is used in some protocols for tick-borne co-infections. It has a long traditional use in West Africa and some laboratory activity against organisms in a dish, which is not the same as evidence that it treats an infection in a person. There are no clinical trials in this use, and it is intensely bitter, which matters if a child has to take it daily.
  • Artemisia needs a careful distinction. Artemisinin, a compound derived from sweet wormwood, is a genuine and important antimalarial drug — a Nobel Prize was awarded for it. That is not the same as herbal Artemisia preparations, which vary in content, and the World Health Organization actively discourages non-pharmaceutical preparations for malaria because inconsistent amounts drive resistance. Separately, Artemisia absinthium — wormwood — contains thujone, which is neurotoxic at higher exposures and carries a seizure risk. Worth knowing precisely which species is in a bottle, and telling the prescribing doctor if there is any seizure history.
  • Japanese knotweed is used within some clinical frameworks for tick-borne illness, usually as a source of resveratrol. There are no clinical trials supporting that use in people, and the general resveratrol literature has been mostly disappointing. It may affect platelets, which matters before surgery or alongside blood thinners. If it is part of a protocol, the questions in judging what you are offered are the ones to ask.
  • A general note on tinctures. Herbal extracts are frequently supplied in alcohol, sometimes at substantial concentrations, and a child on several of them daily may be taking more than anybody has thought about. Glycerine-based versions exist for many of them. Ask, and mention it to the prescribing doctor alongside anything sedating.
  • On specific brands generally: a product being expensive, practitioner-sold, or described as pharmaceutical-grade tells you nothing on its own. What tells you something is third-party testing, which the next section covers.

Binders, drainage and detox products

A whole category within some protocols — charcoal, clays, chlorella, and proprietary blends described as supporting drainage or clearing what treatment has released.

  • The organs that do this already exist. The liver and kidneys clear substances continuously, and mainstream physiology does not recognize the drainage model these products are usually sold within. That does not mean nothing is happening when somebody takes one; it means the explanation offered is not an established one.
  • Evidence for the branded blends is essentially absent. Several widely used products in tick-borne protocols have no clinical trials at all, and are used because a particular protocol includes them. It is fair to ask a practitioner what evidence they are relying on and to expect a straight answer.
  • Binders bind things, and that is the real safety issue. Activated charcoal and clay products are not selective. They will also bind medication and nutrients taken near the same time, potentially making a prescription less effective without anybody realising. If binders are in use, ask specifically how far apart from every medication and from meals they should be given.
  • Watch for constipation, which binders can cause or worsen, in children who often have it already.
  • Be wary of anything positioning worsening symptoms as evidence it is working. That reasoning is covered in judging what you are offered, and it applies here more than almost anywhere.

Where things get more contested

  • Methylated B vitamins generally — as above, there is a lot of loose extrapolation from genetic testing. Ask what specific finding justifies it and what would show whether it is working.
  • Herbal antimicrobial protocols for chronic infection — used within some clinical frameworks and disputed within others. If you are in this territory, the section below on long protocols is written for you.
  • Adaptogenic and calming herbs — widely sold as gentle. Several cause real effects including racing heart, agitation and sedation, and several interact with prescription medication. Individual sensitivity varies enormously, and a bad reaction in one person tells you nothing about the next.

Things that are actively dangerous

Named plainly, because families are offered these and the marketing is persuasive.

Do not, under any circumstances

Chlorine dioxide, MMS, or "miracle mineral" products, including as an enema. This is industrial bleach. It has caused serious injury to children, regulators worldwide have issued warnings, and people have been prosecuted for selling it as a treatment for autism. There is no version of this that is safe.

Chelation for anything other than diagnosed heavy metal poisoning. Children have died. It strips essential minerals along with anything else, and the tests used to justify it are often misleading provoked tests.

Very high dose vitamins, particularly A, D, E, B6 and iron. These accumulate and cause nerve damage, liver injury and worse. "Water-soluble so it's safe" is not true of B6.

Anything requiring you to stop prescribed medication without the prescribing doctor's involvement.

Anything sold with a cure claim, a testimonial-heavy website, urgency, or a practitioner who discourages you from discussing it with your doctor. That last one is the clearest single warning sign there is.

Questions to take with you

  • Here is everything my child takes, including supplements — do any of these interact?
  • Is there a deficiency here we could actually measure before treating?
  • What specifically is this for, and what would improvement look like?
  • How long before we decide whether it is working?
  • What would make you tell me to stop?
  • Is there anything here you would take off the list?
  • Do you sell this, or benefit from my buying it?
  • What is the plan if this does not help?

Print your list from the medication list and hand it over rather than reciting it. Supplements belong on that list alongside prescriptions. That is the whole point of keeping one.

Before you close this

Two things worth doing. Write down everything currently being given, including things bought without advice, and take that list to the next appointment. And ask a pharmacist. They are free, they know interactions better than almost anyone, and hardly anybody asks them.

You are not being difficult by wanting to try something. You are also not failing your child by deciding not to.

Nothing here is medical advice, and no doses appear on this page on purpose. Supplements interact with medication and with each other, and several are harmful in excess. Decisions belong with a licensed medical doctor who knows your child, and anything measurable should be measured before it is treated.

Where evidence is contested, this page says so rather than choosing for you.
