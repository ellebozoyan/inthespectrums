Mealtime communication boards:
words for every step, not just yes and no

Vocabulary built around eating — for a speech-generating device, and as printed boards alongside one. They exist because most food vocabulary lets a person request or refuse, and almost nothing gives them words for what is actually happening in their mouth. The goal throughout is a voice they operate themselves — printed boards support that rather than substitute for it. Why that is the goal, and why it is a professional standard rather than a preference, is set out on the AAC page.

Free, for families and for the people who work with them

Download, print, laminate, load onto a device, hand to a school. No account, no charge, no registration. Adapt them for the person in front of you — that is what they are for.

They are offered as ready-made files and as plain word lists, so they can be rebuilt in whatever system a person already uses.

Why mealtime vocabulary specifically

A communication system can be excellent in general and still leave somebody mute at the table.

Most food vocabulary on most devices does two things: it lets a person ask for something, and it lets them refuse. Both matter. Neither is enough.

What tends to be missing is everything in between — words for what is happening in the mouth right now, words for describing a food rather than accepting it, and words for taking part in a meal socially rather than only transacting through it.

The exploration hierarchy, given words

Feeding therapy works in graded steps: tolerate it nearby, touch it, bring it to the lips, lick, bite, chew, swallow. A person may sit at one step for weeks, and that is progress.

But if the only available words are "eat" and "no", every one of those steps is invisible. The person cannot say what they just did, cannot say what they are willing to do next, and cannot say which part they are stuck on.

So these boards give each step its own word:

tap
touch
smell
lick
bite
chew

blow bubbles

no hands

Several of these are playful on purpose. Blowing bubbles in a drink and making teeth marks in something are real oral-motor steps, and framing them as play rather than as eating lowers the stakes much.

Refusing well, which is a skill

A person who can only refuse by pushing a plate away, leaving the table, or becoming distressed has been given no better option. Explicit refusal vocabulary is not permission to refuse more — it is a way to refuse that does not cost them anything.

no thank you
all done
not yet
too much
take it away
I need help
stop
Oh no!

Honor it every time. A refusal word that is overridden teaches faster than any of the rest of this, and what it teaches is that the board does not work.

Commentary, which is what makes it a meal

Requesting is a transaction. Commenting is company. A person who can say delicious, that's funny, more, or describe food is at dinner rather than being fed at it — and that difference is most of the point.

delicious!
that's funny
more
I like it
it's my favorite
look!
yuck
let's talk about it

Available does not mean prompted

These words exist so the person can use them. Model them yourself, often, without requiring a response — point to bite as you take one, hit delicious when something is.

Do not turn the board into a demand. A device used mainly to make somebody request food becomes another thing happening to them at the table, and mealtimes already have enough of that. See speech, language and AAC on modeling without prompting.

Using them at the table

The general guidance on building communication into a day — at home, in therapy, at school — lives on the speech, language and AAC page. This is the mealtime-specific part.

The single practice that matters most

Model it constantly, and expect nothing back. Use the device or board yourself while you talk — several times a meal, for months — without asking the person to respond and without turning it into a question.

Meals are unusually good for this. They happen several times a day, every day, and few activities offer that much natural practice.

At mealtimes specifically

Where these particular boards do their work.

  • The device is on the table, every meal, like cutlery
  • An activity board sits beside it, never instead of it
  • Model the exploration words as you eat — hit bite as you take one
  • Honor every refusal, immediately and visibly
  • Nobody is asked to request their food before receiving it
  • Comment on the food yourself, often, expecting no reply

Why it matters hereMeals happen several times a day, every day. Few activities offer that much natural practice.

What to model, specifically

Words that fit what is already happening.

  • Hit bite as you take one, delicious when something is
  • All done when you finish, not only when they should
  • Describe food then a property — crunchy, cold, too hot
  • More, not yet, I don't like this about your own plate
  • Narrate the steps: smell it first, just a lick

The pointThey need to hear the language spoken before they can be expected to use it.

Three things that should never happen

At the table or anywhere else.

  • Never take it away — not as a consequence, not to encourage speech, not because a meal is going badly
  • Never require it to earn food. Making somebody request what they are entitled to teaches that communication is a toll
  • Never ignore a message you did not want. A refusal overridden teaches, in one moment, that the whole system is decorative

Why hereMealtimes are where all three happen most.

The word lists exist deliberately. A person's own system is nearly always better than a new one, and a good speech therapist can build these into an existing vocabulary in an afternoon.

Using them well

  • Put it where meals happen, permanently. A board fetched from a drawer when somebody is expected to communicate is a demand; a board that lives on the table is an option.
  • Model without requiring. Use it yourself, several times a meal, expecting nothing back.
  • Keep positions stable. Moving buttons to make room costs more than the new word is worth. Motor memory is doing much of the work.
  • Both at once is fine. A printed board at the table and a device in the bag is a reasonable arrangement — devices run out of battery, and boards do not.
  • Never withhold access to it. Not as a consequence, not to encourage speech, not because a meal is going badly. Removing somebody's words is not a behavior strategy.
  • Everybody at the table should be able to use it, including siblings. It becomes ordinary far faster that way.

One thing that is often got backwards

Giving somebody a way to refuse food does not make them eat less. The evidence runs the other way — people who can reliably decline tend, over time, to try more, because attempting something has stopped being a trap they cannot get out of.

The same holds for speech. Providing communication support does not suppress talking; it consistently either helps or makes no difference. See speech, language and AAC.

Access to a device they operate themselves is the goal, and no test score or proof of readiness may be required first — see speech, language and AAC for why, and for what to say when you are told otherwise.

These boards are a starting point, not a prescription. Delete what is irrelevant, add what matters to your person, change the words to the ones your family actually uses. A board with fifteen words somebody uses beats a board with sixty they do not.

Reading this as a professional

The exploration vocabulary is the part worth borrowing. Graded steps are standard in feeding work, and giving each step its own word lets a person self-report where they are in the hierarchy — which is otherwise inferred by the adult watching them.

Refusal and commentary vocabulary are included deliberately. Requesting-only sets are still common, and they teach that communication is for obtaining things.

Nothing here is clinical advice. A speech and language therapist who knows the person should be involved in any communication system, and these boards are designed to be adapted rather than adopted whole.
