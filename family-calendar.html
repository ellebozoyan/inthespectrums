The family calendar:
one day, on one screen

The routine, the medication, the meals and the appointments in one place — with a big, plain view you can leave open on a tablet on the kitchen wall, and a simpler version for the person themselves that shows only what you choose to share.

How the syncing works, honestly

This tool does not have a server, which is why nobody can see your family's schedule. That also means it cannot sync between phones on its own.

So it hands the job to the calendars you already have. Build the schedule here, download a calendar file, and import it into Apple Calendar or Google Calendar. Those already sync across every device you own — and they already let you share a calendar with a partner, a grandparent, a school or a therapist, with different people seeing different calendars.

That path is free, works on iPhone and Android, and needs no new account. Using these tools covers how to leave this page open full-screen on a wall tablet.

Put it on everyone's phones

Download a calendar file and import it into Apple Calendar or Google Calendar. From there it syncs to every device and can be shared with whoever you choose.

This is the only copy. Everything lives in the browser on the device that you are currently using — not in an account and not in the cloud. Clearing your browsing data, changing browser, or losing the device loses it.

Bring in a calendar you already have

If the schedule already lives in Apple Calendar, Google Calendar or Outlook, export it and load it here rather than typing it again.

How to export what you have. In Apple Calendar on a Mac: select the calendar, then File → Export. In Google Calendar on a computer: Settings → Import and export → Export, which gives a zip — unzip it and use the .ics inside. In Outlook: File → Save Calendar. On a phone this is usually easier done on a computer first.

Nothing is uploaded. The file is read in your browser and never leaves the device.

How to import it

iPhone or Mac
Open the downloaded file and choose which calendar to add it to. Make a calendar called something like "Ellie's day" first, so it can be shared and turned off separately. In Calendar on a Mac, right-click that calendar and choose Share Calendar to add a partner or a caregiver.

Android or Google
On a computer, open Google Calendar, then Settings → Import and export → Import, and pick the file and the calendar. Share it from Settings for that calendar → Share with specific people, and choose whether they can see details or only free/busy.

Re-download and re-import when things change. Deleting the old imported calendar first avoids duplicates.

Give each adult only what they need

The reason to export several small calendars rather than one large one.

If somebody's phone buzzes for every dose of medication they are not there to give, they will silence the whole calendar within a week — and then miss the appointment that actually needed them. The fix is not fewer reminders overall. It is different reminders for different people.

So export more than once, changing the Include setting each time, and import each file as its own named calendar. Everyone then subscribes only to the ones that concern them, and can mute or unsubscribe from the rest without losing anything else.

What the simple view shows

The view meant for the person themselves, on a wall tablet or their own device.

  • Only items you ticked as shareable, plus meals
  • Larger type, no notes, no medication detail unless you tick it
  • What is happening now is highlighted, and what has passed is dimmed
  • No diagnoses, no appointment reasons, no adult notes — ever

If a person is old enough to want more detail, give it to them. This is a starting point, not a rule — see their own voice.

A visible schedule does more than organize a day. Knowing what is coming is one of the most reliable ways to reduce the anxiety that drives hard moments — most of what looks like resistance is a response to being surprised. See in the moment on previewing and transitions.

Reading this as a professional

If a family shares this with you, the simple view is deliberately filtered — what a person sees about their own day is a decision their family has made, and it will change as they get older.

The weekly pattern is often more informative than any account of a typical day. Look at the density, the transitions, and how much of the week is already spoken for before anything you add.

Nothing here is medical advice. Medication times shown are drawn from the family's own list and are not a prescription. Verify against the label and the prescribing doctor.
