Myofunctional therapy:
the mouth has five jobs, and they're connected

Orofacial myofunctional therapy works on the muscles and habits of the face, mouth, and tongue — how a person breathes, rests their tongue, chews, swallows, and speaks. It's one of the least known therapies on this site, and for the right child it addresses several problems that were being treated separately by people who never spoke to each other.

Why one therapy touches so many things

The same set of muscles and the same resting posture are involved in all five of these. Change the pattern and you're not treating one of them — you're treating the system they share.

BreathingThrough the nose, lips together, at rest and asleep
SleepingAirway open, no snoring, no mouth breathing
EatingChewing efficiently, managing texture, a mature swallow
DrinkingLip seal, controlled sipping, no spillage
SpeakingTongue placement for sounds, clarity, endurance

The unifying question is where the tongue lives and how the person breathes. A tongue that rests low and forward, and a mouth that stays open, changes swallowing, changes how the jaw and palate grow, changes how sounds are formed, and changes what happens to the airway at night. This is why families sometimes find that a single referral answers questions they'd been carrying to four different specialists.

What it actually is

Physical therapy for the mouth and face — assessment, then a set of small daily exercises, practiced over months.

A therapist assesses resting tongue and lip posture, nasal versus mouth breathing, the swallow pattern, chewing, oral structure including any tethered tissue, and habits like thumb sucking or prolonged pacifier use. From there comes a program of short exercises — usually a few minutes, a couple of times a day — retraining the muscles and then the automatic patterns they produce.

Courses typically run several months to a year. Daily home practice is the single biggest determinant of whether it works, more than the specific protocol or the specific therapist, which is worth knowing before you commit a family to it.

Touch cues and hands-on facilitation

Worth knowing about, because it's a skill families often struggle to find. Myofunctional work is hands-in-and-around-the-mouth by nature, and the training teaches tactile stimulation and oral placement techniques as a cueing system in their own right — so clinicians in this discipline tend to be practiced and comfortable with orofacial touch.

That matters for speech as well as for the muscle work. Some people cannot reach a sound by hearing it or watching a mouth produce it; they need to feel where the jaw, lips, and tongue go. A clinician placing hands on the jaw or lips to shape that movement is supplying information no amount of modeling can.

Myofunctional clinicians are one of two groups most likely to have this in their hands — the other being motor speech specialists trained in approaches such as PROMPT. If touch cues are something you're looking for, ask both. The speech, language and AAC page covers what to ask, and the consent and sensory considerations that come first.

Who provides it

Most commonly speech-language pathologists with additional myofunctional training, and also some dentists, dental hygienists, and occupational therapists. There's a certification for orofacial myology, and asking what training a practitioner holds is a fair and useful question. Many myofunctional therapists work closely with ENT, orthodontics, dentistry, sleep medicine, and feeding therapy — and the good ones will tell you when your child needs one of those first.

Signs that make it fair to ask about

  • Mouth breathing during the day, or sleeping with the mouth open
  • Snoring, restless sleep, or waking unrefreshed
  • Tongue visible between the teeth at rest or during swallowing
  • Messy or noisy eating, food pocketing, slow chewing, swallowing food whole
  • Drooling past the age you'd expect
  • Speech sound errors involving tongue placement, particularly s and z sounds
  • A narrow palate, crowded teeth, or an open bite
  • Orthodontic work that has relapsed after braces came off
  • Thumb sucking or pacifier use continuing past the toddler years
  • Chapped lips, dark circles, or a habitually forward head posture

What the evidence supports, and what it doesn't

This field has enthusiastic advocates and a research base that is real but uneven. Sorting the claims by strength is more useful than either dismissing it or accepting all of it.

Reasonable support

Retraining a tongue-thrust swallow and correcting resting tongue posture. Supporting speech sound work where tongue placement is the barrier. Stabilizing orthodontic results and reducing relapse. Eliminating sucking habits.

As an adjunct in pediatric sleep-disordered breathing, alongside ENT and dental treatment rather than instead of them, there is evidence of modest benefit.

Plausible, still emerging

Improving chewing efficiency and feeding in children with oral motor difficulty. Reducing drooling. Rehabilitation after a frenectomy — widely practiced, though the frenectomy field itself has genuine internal disagreement about who needs the procedure at all.

Effects on facial growth and palate development, where the theory is coherent and the long-term outcome data is thinner than the confidence with which it's often presented.

Treat with caution

Claims that myofunctional therapy alone resolves obstructive sleep apnea, or replaces ENT surgery, orthodontics, or CPAP where those are indicated.

Claims extending to attention, behavior, posture, or general health outcomes. Packages bundling an expensive tongue-tie release with a mandatory therapy course sold together.

The practical version

If a child snores, mouth breathes, or has disrupted sleep, get the airway assessed first — ENT, and sleep medicine if indicated. Enlarged tonsils and adenoids are the most common cause of pediatric sleep apnea, and no amount of tongue exercise addresses them. Myofunctional therapy has a real role alongside that work, and sometimes afterward to consolidate it. It is not a substitute for finding out what's blocking the airway.

When the structure itself is in the way

Sometimes the muscles are being asked to work around anatomy that doesn't give them room. Exercise cannot fix a blocked nose or a tongue that cannot reach the palate — and it's good to know what to look at before committing to months of therapy.

Two things that change what's possible

  • Tethered oral tissues. A tight band under the tongue or lip that limits movement. These are commonly described in grades, roughly by how far forward the attachment sits and how much movement is restricted. What matters clinically is not the grade on its own but what the tongue and lip can actually do — whether the tongue can lift to the palate, sweep the cheeks, and stay up at rest. A characteristic dipped or heart-shaped look when the tongue lifts is one sign that restriction is functional rather than cosmetic.
  • Nasal obstruction. Enlarged turbinates, a deviated septum, allergic swelling, or large adenoids can make nasal breathing genuinely hard. A child who cannot breathe through their nose will keep their mouth open and their tongue low and forward, because that is the only way to get air. That posture then affects the swallow, the resting position that shapes the palate, and the placement available for speech sounds.

Why the two together matter more than either alone

A restricted tongue and a blocked nose reinforce each other. The nose pushes the mouth open; the tether keeps the tongue down. Between them, the resting posture that myofunctional therapy is trying to establish may not be physically available — and a child can work diligently at exercises they are structurally unable to perform.

This is why a careful assessment looks at both, and why relieving an obstruction sometimes changes what therapy can achieve rather than replacing the need for it.

Where the evidence sits, honestly

  • Release of tethered tissue for speech is genuinely contested. The evidence is strongest for infant feeding difficulty, thinner and more disputed for speech outcomes, and reviews are generally cautious. Some children improve markedly afterward; the research does not currently let anyone promise that in advance.
  • Treating real nasal obstruction is well established as ENT practice on its own merits — for breathing, sleep, and quality of life — separately from any speech claim.
  • Therapy before and after matters. Where release is done, structured myofunctional work is standard practice to build the new movement patterns. Releasing tissue without the follow-up rehabilitation is widely regarded as leaving the job half done.
  • Assess function, not just anatomy. A visible tether that isn't limiting anything does not need treating. What justifies intervention is a documented functional problem — feeding, breathing, sleep, oral hygiene, or speech — that the restriction plausibly explains.

Before agreeing to a procedure

Get an opinion from someone who does not perform the release. That single step is the best protection available in an area with real financial incentives and genuine professional disagreement.

Then ask directly: what specific function is limited, how will we measure whether it improved, what does the aftercare involve and for how long, and what happens if there is no change? A clinician who can answer those four questions is worth listening to whichever way they advise.

What often improves when structure and function are both addressed

The reason this sits on a myofunctional page rather than a surgical one is that the gains families describe are rarely confined to a single area. Where a genuine restriction is relieved and therapy follows, the changes reported tend to span the whole set of jobs the mouth does:

  • Breathing — nasal rather than through the mouth, awake and asleep
  • Sleep — quieter, less restless, waking more rested
  • Eating — more efficient chewing, fewer textures refused, faster meals
  • Drinking — better lip seal, less spillage, cleaner straw and cup use
  • Oral hygiene — tooth brushing and dental visits that are physically easier to tolerate
  • Speech — clearer placement for sounds that need the tongue against the palate

Worth holding two things at once: these outcomes are real for some children and are not guaranteed for any child. The honest framing to ask a clinician for is which of the six they expect to change, and by when.

For a child with additional needs

Standard protocols assume a person who can follow verbal instruction, imitate a movement on request, and practice daily without a fight. That describes some children and not others.

What to ask before starting

  • Have you worked with children who can't follow verbal directions? A skilled therapist adapts through play, mirrors, food, music, and modeling rather than abandoning the work — but not everyone can.
  • How will this fit a child with oral sensory sensitivity? Exercises involving touch in and around the mouth are a genuine barrier for many children and need to be built up slowly, or the whole program collapses in week two.
  • What does the home program look like realistically? If it needs ten cooperative minutes twice a day, and your household has none, say so at the assessment rather than failing quietly for six months.
  • What comes first? If a child is not eating enough, aspirating, or in an acute airway situation, that outranks this. Ask directly what the sequence should be.
  • How will we know it's working, and when would you stop? Ask for the specific functional change you're aiming at and a date to reassess.

Where it can help

For children with low tone, feeding difficulty, or chronic mouth breathing, the targets here — lip seal, tongue posture, chewing, nasal breathing — are functional and matter daily. Better nasal breathing supports better sleep. Better sleep supports nearly everything else on this site.

It also tends to be one of the few therapies where progress is visible to a family at home, which is worth something on its own.

Practical matters

Finding a practitioner

  • Look for a speech-language pathologist with specific orofacial myofunctional training, or a practitioner holding orofacial myology certification.
  • Ask who they coordinate with. A myofunctional therapist who never refers out to ENT, dentistry, or sleep medicine is a warning sign, not a convenience.
  • Ask about experience with your child's specific profile — sensory sensitivity, developmental disability, feeding difficulty, or limited speech.
  • Ask what happens if the exercises don't stick. A good answer involves adapting the approach; a poor one involves blaming compliance.

Paying for it

  • Coverage varies widely and a fair amount of this work is out of pocket. When it is delivered by an SLP and billed under speech therapy codes it is sometimes covered — verify by code before starting, as described on the therapy funding page.
  • A letter of medical necessity tied to a documented functional problem — sleep-disordered breathing, feeding difficulty, speech intelligibility — is far more likely to be paid than a general request for myofunctional therapy.
  • Ask about session frequency and total expected duration upfront so you can price the whole course rather than the first visit.
One last thing

Three questions carry most of this: what outcome are we aiming at, how will we know it is working, and by when.

Ask those in any first appointment and you are already ahead of most people walking through the door — including on the days you feel like you have no idea what you are doing.

This is a map, not medical advice. Whether myofunctional therapy is appropriate for a particular child, and what should be treated first, is a decision for clinicians who have examined them. Airway symptoms in children warrant medical assessment.

Where the evidence is still developing, this page says so. That isn't a reason to avoid the therapy — it's a reason to ask what outcome is expected, how it will be measured, and by when.
