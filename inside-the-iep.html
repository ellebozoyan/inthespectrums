Inside the IEP:
what the document actually does

The programs page covers what exists. This page covers what happens in the room — how to read a goal, what the words mean, and the one section of the document that belongs entirely to you.

Reading this from the other side of the table

This page is written to help a family be effective in a meeting you may be sitting in — which is worth naming honestly rather than pretending otherwise.

Most school professionals want the same outcome and are working inside constraints they didn't choose. A parent who arrives with specific, documented, well-phrased requests makes your job easier, not harder: it is far simpler to advocate internally for something a family has asked for clearly and in writing than for something everyone senses but nobody has stated.

If you can only do one thing with this page, tell families that the parent concerns section is theirs to write, and that it should be submitted in advance.

Start here

Write every concern into the parent concerns statement. Every single one.

Parent concerns is the only part of the IEP that you write. Everything else is drafted by the district in the district's language. This section goes into the legal document itself, and it is the record of what you raised and when you raised it.

The rule is simple and it has consequences for years: if it isn't written there, it didn't happen. You cannot later argue that a district failed to address a concern you never put in writing. A concern raised in the hallway, mentioned in passing during the meeting, or said warmly to a teacher who agreed with you does not exist anywhere the system can see.

Why every concern, including the ones that seem off-topic

  • It creates a dated record. Years later, in a dispute, at a placement decision, or when a new team arrives who never met your child, the concerns statement is the evidence of what you said and when.
  • It forces a response. When you raise a concern and the district declines to act on it, that generally triggers a written explanation of the refusal — a formal notice stating what they're refusing and why. That document is enormously valuable and you only get it if you asked in the first place.
  • Home behavior is educationally relevant, whatever you're told. A child who holds it together all day and falls apart at four o'clock is telling you something about their school day. Write down the four o'clock. It is evidence of the cost of the current program, and it belongs in the record even if the team says it's outside their scope.
  • Small things become patterns. A concern that looks minor this year is the third data point in a documented pattern two years from now — but only if you wrote it down each time.
  • Nobody else will write it. Teams summarize, paraphrase, and soften. Your words are the only place your version survives intact.

Write comprehensively, discuss selectively

The honest caveat: a twenty-item list read aloud will consume the meeting and dilute the two things that matter most this year. So do both. Put everything in the written statement — that's the permanent record and it costs you nothing. Then choose two or three to actually push on in the room, and say so out loud: "I've submitted my full concerns in writing; today I want to focus on communication and safety."

Comprehensive on paper. Focused in the conversation. Those are not in tension.

How to submit it so it survives

  1. Write it yourself, before the meeting — not in the room, not dictated to someone typing.
  2. Email it to the team several days ahead, dated, and keep the sent message. Ask that it be attached and entered verbatim.
  3. Say the word verbatim. Districts sometimes summarize parent concerns into two sentences. You can decline that. Ask for your statement in full, in your words.
  4. Check the draft and the final document to confirm it's actually in there and hasn't been edited. This step gets skipped and it's the one that matters.
  5. If it was shortened or altered, email a correction asking that your original be attached to the record, and keep that email too.
  6. There is a real question about timing, and advocates differ. Some prefer submitting after the meeting, so the statement can include what actually came up. Others submit beforehand, so the team has read it before decisions get made. Both are defensible, and the answer below is the one that gets most of each.
  7. Update it every year. Don't copy last year's — write what's true now, and note what's still unresolved from last time. The phrase "as I also raised in [month, year]" is quietly powerful.

Before the meeting, after it, or both

The case for beforehand: it shapes the agenda rather than responding to it. Nobody can later say the concern was never raised. And it goes into the record before the draft document is written, which is when it can still influence what the draft says.

The case for afterward: the meeting always produces something you did not know when you sat down — a new assessment result, a proposal you want to respond to, something said out loud that you want on paper. A statement written afterward is more complete and more accurate.

The version that gets most of both: submit a full statement several days beforehand, and say in it that a supplement will follow.

"These are our concerns as of today. We will submit a short supplement after the meeting reflecting anything that arises there, and we ask that both documents be attached to the IEP verbatim."

One word of caution about calling it a draft. A document labeled DRAFT can be treated as not yet real — not circulated, not read, not attached. What you have written is not a draft; it is complete as of the date you wrote it. Framing it as a complete statement plus a supplement is stronger than framing it as an unfinished one, and it commits you to the same follow-up.

Say it out loud at the meeting too. Something like: "I sent our concerns on the 8th. I will send a short supplement within a few days covering what we have discussed today, and I am asking that both be attached verbatim." Getting that into the meeting means it is in the notes, and it sets the expectation before the document is finalized.

Then actually send it, quickly. There are usually timelines for issuing the IEP after a meeting, so a supplement arriving three weeks later may arrive after the document is done. A few days is the window. Short is fine — a supplement can be three sentences.

If you have an advocate, follow their advice on this. They know your district, and how a particular team receives things is not a small factor.

How to read a goal

Most of an IEP's real content sits in the goals, and most goals are written vaguely enough to be unenforceable. This is the skill that changes what your child actually receives.

Unenforceable

Student will improve communication skills as measured by teacher observation.

No baseline, so nobody knows where he started. No condition, so it could mean anything anywhere. No number, so any outcome counts as progress. Measured by observation, which means measured by opinion.

You cannot tell whether this goal was met, which means nobody can be held to it.

Enforceable

Given a familiar routine and his speech device within reach, Student will independently initiate a request or comment in 4 out of 5 opportunities across 3 consecutive data sessions, as measured by SLP data logs. Baseline: 1 in 5 with adult prompting.

Baseline stated. Conditions named. A number that can be checked. A named person collecting data in a named format.

At the next review you can ask to see the logs, and the answer is a fact rather than an impression.

The five questions to ask about any goal

  1. What's the baseline? Without it, progress is unmeasurable. Ask for it in the document, not in conversation.
  2. Under what conditions? With prompting or without? In a quiet room or the classroom? With the device or without? These change everything.
  3. How will it be measured, by whom, how often? "Teacher observation" is the weakest possible answer. Ask for data.
  4. Is this actually the priority? A goal about handwriting when the child can't yet ask for help is a misallocation, however well written.
  5. What happens if there's no progress? Ask for that written in: at what point do we change the approach rather than repeat the goal next year?

The single most common problem

The same goal appearing three years running with the numbers slightly adjusted. That is not a child failing to progress — it is a program that hasn't changed. When you see it, say so plainly: this is the third year of this goal. What is going to be different this time? And ask for the answer in the document.

Words that mean something specific

A handful of terms carry most of the weight. Knowing them is the difference between agreeing to something and agreeing to nothing.

Direct vs. consult minutes

Direct service means a specialist works with your child. Consultation means they advise the teacher. Both appear as service on the page and they are entirely different things.

Watch forServices quietly shifting from direct to consult at an annual review. Ask specifically which is being proposed and why.

As needed / as available

These phrases make a service optional. "Speech as needed" commits the district to nothing at all.

Ask forSpecific minutes, a specific frequency, and a specific setting. Numbers are enforceable; adjectives are not.

Accommodation vs. modification

An accommodation changes how a child accesses the same content — extra time, audio, a scribe. A modification changes what they're expected to learn.

Matters becauseModifications can affect diploma track and post-secondary options. Ask about long-term implications before agreeing.

Least restrictive environment

The legal requirement that a child be educated with peers who do not have disabilities, to the greatest extent appropriate. It is a presumption, not an absolute — the right placement is the one where the child can actually learn.

NoteThis works both ways. It's used to argue against separate placement and can be answered with evidence about what a child needs to make progress.

Prior written notice

When a district proposes or refuses a change, it must give you written notice explaining what and why, and what information it relied on. This is the paper trail for any later dispute.

Use itWhen told no, ask for that refusal in writing. It is your right and it changes the tone of the conversation immediately.

Stay put

During a formal dispute, the current placement and services generally continue unchanged while it's resolved.

Matters becauseIt means a district cannot unilaterally reduce services while you're contesting the change.

Independent educational evaluation

If you disagree with the district's evaluation, you can request an independent one, potentially at public expense.

NoteKnowing this exists changes conversations even when you don't use it.

Extended school year

Services over the summer, based on whether a child regresses and how long recovery takes — not on whether they'd benefit generally.

Build the caseDocument skill loss over breaks. That data is what decides it, and only you can collect it.

How services are actually delivered

The same number of minutes can mean very different things. These are the words on the grid, and what each one costs and buys.

Pull-out

The person leaves the classroom and works with the therapist, alone or in a small group.

  • Quieter, fewer distractions, easier to teach a new skill
  • Easier for the therapist to work on something specific
  • But: they miss whatever was happening in class
  • And skills learned in a quiet room often do not transfer out of it

AskWhat is being missed, and how does this skill get practiced in the classroom afterward?

Push-in

The therapist comes into the classroom and works there, often alongside other children.

  • Skills are practiced where they are actually needed
  • The teacher sees the strategies and can continue them
  • Nothing is missed, and it is less conspicuous
  • But: noisier, and harder to teach something genuinely new

The usual answerBoth. Pull-out to teach it, push-in to make it real. Neither alone tends to be enough.

Dyad and small group

Two children, or a few, working with the therapist together.

  • Genuinely the right format for social communication and pragmatics — you cannot practice conversation alone
  • Peer modeling, and often more motivating
  • But: it is also cheaper, so it is sometimes chosen for that reason

Fair to askWho is the group partner, why that pairing, and what is being worked on that needs another person?

Consultation

The specialist advises and trains others rather than working with the person directly.

  • Done well, it spreads expertise across the whole day
  • Done badly, it is a plan in a folder
  • The difference is almost entirely supervision

Get it namedDirect or consultative, how many minutes, and by whom — more on this distinction.

The line that matters on the service grid

"Speech: 60 minutes weekly" is ambiguous. Sixty minutes with the therapist, sixty minutes in a group of four, and sixty minutes of the therapist advising an aide are three different services described identically.

A grid should say, for each service: direct or consultative, individual or group, how many minutes, how often, in what setting, and delivered by whom. If any of that is missing, it is reasonable to ask for it to be specified, and a change to any of it is a change to the plan rather than an internal scheduling matter.

Adults in the room, and where the room is

One-to-one support

  • Usually a paraprofessional or aide, not a certified teacher or therapist. Training varies enormously, and turnover is high nearly everywhere.
  • The purpose should be written down. Safety, access to instruction, behavioral support and personal care are different jobs needing different training.
  • There is a real trade-off. Constant adult proximity can reduce peer interaction, increase learned dependence, and mean the person is never approached by another child. Good one-to-one support fades deliberately; poor support hovers.
  • Ask what the fading plan is from the outset. If there is not one, the support is not being designed toward independence.
  • Ask who trains and supervises them, and what happens when they change.

Where the program sits

  • In-district — the neighborhood school or another school in the same district. Keeps the person near home and near siblings and neighbors, and is usually the starting assumption.
  • Integrated or inclusion classes — general education with support built in, sometimes co-taught by a general and a special educator. The legal expectation across the US is education alongside non-disabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate, with removal only when it genuinely cannot work with supports.
  • Substantially separate classrooms — smaller, more specialized, less access to typical peers. Appropriate for some people and over-used for others.
  • Regional programs and collaboratives — several districts pooling resources to run specialist programs no single district could sustain. Depending on the state these are called collaboratives, intermediate units, service units, or boards of cooperative educational services. Often excellent, and worth visiting.
  • Approved private special education schools — independent schools that states approve to serve students whose needs the district cannot meet. Where the district cannot provide an appropriate program, it is responsible for the cost, including transportation. This is not a favor and it is not charity; it is what the obligation requires when the alternative is inadequate.
  • Residential placement — rare, for the most intensive needs, and a decision with a great deal attached to it.

How placement is supposed to be decided

The order matters, and it is frequently reversed in practice. Needs are determined first, then goals, then services, then the setting where those services can be delivered. Placement is the last step, not the first.

Which means "we do not have that program here" is a description of the district’s current offerings, not a determination of what a person needs. If the team concludes the district cannot deliver it, the obligation does not disappear — it moves.

Most district staff are working within genuine constraints and are not being obstructive. Naming the sequence calmly is usually more effective than treating it as a fight.

Time of year

  • Extended school year — services continuing beyond the standard year. This is not summer school and not enrichment; it is part of the entitlement for those who qualify.
  • The usual standard is regression and recoupment — whether skills are lost over a break and how long they take to recover. Many states also allow other grounds, including a skill at a critical stage of being learned, an emerging skill that a break would interrupt, or the severity of need.
  • Data decides it, so collect some. Skills recorded before and after a long break are the strongest argument there is, and families are rarely told to gather them. The symptom tracker and goals tracker both do this.
  • It does not have to look like the school year. Extended school year should be based on what the person needs to maintain, which may be fewer hours, fewer services, or a different setting.
  • Year-round and extended-day programs exist in some districts and specialist schools, and can suit someone for whom long breaks are genuinely costly.

When behavior is part of the picture

The functional behavioral assessment

  • You can ask for one, in writing, at any time. A functional behavioral assessment is an investigation into what a behavior is achieving for the person — what happens before it, what follows it, and what need it is meeting. It is the step before writing a behavior plan.
  • The general expectation across the US is that where behavior impedes a person’s learning or the learning of others, the team must consider positive behavioral supports and strategies to address it. Assessment is also required in specific disciplinary situations — particularly where removals from school add up to a change of placement.
  • A plan written without an assessment is guesswork. Professional standards for behavior analysts require that intervention be based on assessment — which means direct observation of the actual behavior, in the settings where it happens, not a plan built from a description at a meeting.
  • So ask directly: has the person who wrote this plan observed the behavior themselves? Where, when, and for how long? A behavior seen only in one setting, or only once, has not been understood.
  • Ask what function was identified and what the plan does about that function. If a plan only lists what staff will do when it happens, it is a response plan rather than a behavior plan.
  • If a request for assessment is refused, ask for the refusal in writing with the reasons. In the US districts must give written notice when they decline to evaluate, and that notice is what makes the decision reviewable.

What a good behavior plan contains

What the behavior is, in observable terms. What tends to precede it. What it appears to achieve. What will change in the environment so it is needed less. What will be taught instead. What adults will do, consistently. How progress will be measured, and when the plan will be reviewed.

And what happens in the ten minutes before — including whether the person has just been talked about in front of, which is a common and almost invisible trigger. See their own voice.

Rights that are easy to miss

Placement stays put while a dispute is open

  • In the US, while a due process proceeding is pending, the person generally remains in their current educational placement unless the family and the district agree otherwise. This is often called stay put, or pendency.
  • What that means in practice: a district cannot move someone out of a program during a dispute simply by deciding to. Current placement holds while it is resolved.
  • It cuts both ways. If a family wants a new placement and files, stay put generally keeps the person where they are during the process — so it protects continuity rather than guaranteeing the outcome you want.
  • There are exceptions, including some involving weapons, drugs or serious bodily injury, and this is an area where the detail matters and varies. If it is live for you, get advice from someone who knows your state.

Everything you write becomes part of the record

  • Assume every email, note and message is read later, by people you have not met. Educational records can be requested by families, and disputes are frequently decided on the paper trail.
  • Write for that reader. Dated, factual, specific, and calm. "On the 14th he came home with a torn shirt and I have not had an explanation" carries considerably more weight than three paragraphs of frustration, and the frustration may be entirely justified.
  • This is also the reason to put things in writing at all. A conversation in a corridor did not happen, as far as any later process is concerned. A short email confirming what was said does.
  • Request your records periodically, not only when something goes wrong — families are often surprised by what is in them, and it is far easier to correct something early.
  • Not everything is guaranteed to be included. What counts as an educational record has limits and is interpreted differently in different places. Keep your own copies of everything you send and receive.

The frustration problem, said plainly

Families in dispute are sometimes characterized as difficult, unreasonable, or impossible to work with — and once that framing takes hold it colors how every subsequent request is received, and it can appear in the record.

This is not fair, and it is not a reason to stop advocating. But it is real, and knowing it exists is worth more than being told to stay positive.

What helps: put requests in writing rather than raising them in the moment. Ask questions rather than making accusations. Name what you want to happen next rather than only what went wrong. Bring somebody with you to difficult meetings, both for support and as a witness. And when you are too angry to write well — which is a reasonable thing to be — write it, then send it tomorrow.

None of this means being agreeable when something is wrong. It means keeping the focus on the facts, because the facts are usually the strongest thing you have.

Before keeping a child out of school

When school is going badly, keeping the person home can feel like the only protective option available. Sometimes there is genuinely no alternative for a period. But it is worth understanding what it can set in motion, because families are rarely told.

  • Absence itself becomes an explanation. If progress is limited, missed school is a straightforward account of why — and it can weaken a later argument that the program was inadequate.
  • Attendance is enforced. Extended unexcused absence can lead to truancy proceedings, and in some places to a referral alleging educational neglect. That these outcomes are unjust in a situation where school has genuinely become unsafe does not stop them happening.
  • Withdrawing changes your legal footing. In most US states, formally withdrawing to homeschool substantially changes or ends the entitlement to services under an IEP. What is available afterward varies a great deal by state, and is generally less.
  • Compensatory claims get harder when the person was not there to receive what was offered.

Better routes, where they exist: ask in writing for an urgent team meeting. Ask what would need to change for attendance to be possible. Request a shortened day, a different setting, or home or hospital instruction as a documented interim arrangement rather than an informal absence. Get medical documentation if there is a medical reason. Ask for an independent evaluation. Speak to a special education advocate or attorney before withdrawing, not afterward.

If the person is genuinely unsafe, act — and document why, in writing, at the time. The record of your reasoning matters as much as the decision.

Rules differ by country, and within the US by state, and they change. Nothing here is legal advice. Your state’s parent training and information center is free, funded to explain exactly this, and independent of your district — they are the first call worth making.

The meeting itself

Before

  • Ask for the draft at least a few days ahead and read it. Teams often arrive with a document mostly written; you want to see it before the room does.
  • Submit your concerns statement in writing, dated, ahead of time.
  • Bring someone — a partner, a friend, an advocate. A second person changes the dynamic and takes notes while you talk.
  • Check your state's rules on recording the meeting; where permitted, notice is usually required.
  • Decide your two or three priorities in advance and write them at the top of your page.

During

  • You are a full member of the team, not a guest being briefed. You can ask questions, disagree, and slow things down.
  • You do not have to sign at the meeting. Take the document home. This is almost always the right move and nobody will tell you it's an option.
  • Ask for anything agreed verbally to be written into the document before you leave. Verbal agreements evaporate at staff changes.
  • When you don't understand a term, say so. Half the room may not either.
  • If it becomes clear more time is needed, ask to reconvene rather than agreeing under time pressure.

After

  • Send a summary email of what you understood was agreed. It's polite, and it timestamps the record.
  • Read the final document against your notes, and flag discrepancies in writing.
  • You can consent to parts of an IEP and reject others in many states — ask how partial consent works where you live.
  • Diary a check-in at three months. Don't wait a year to discover a service isn't being delivered.

The tone that works

Warm, specific, documented, and immovable on the two things that matter. Most school teams want to do right by your child and are working inside constraints they didn't choose. Treating them as adversaries from the start costs you allies; treating the process as informal costs you everything else.

Be the parent who is easy to work with and impossible to fob off. Those are compatible, and the combination is unusual enough to be effective.

Before the meeting

Everything on this page is technique. It can be learned in an afternoon, and you have most of it now.

What you bring that nobody else in that room has is the whole of your child — years of it, the parts that never made it into a report. That is the part that matters, and you already have it. Write it down, say it plainly, and let the technique carry it.

This is a map, not legal advice. Special education procedure varies by state — timelines, partial consent, recording rules, and dispute processes all differ. Your state's parent training and information center gives free, accurate, local guidance, and it is the first call worth making.

Keep everything. Date everything. Write the concerns down.
