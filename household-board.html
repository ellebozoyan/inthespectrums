The household board:
contributions, jobs, and saving for something

Not a sticker chart. Two separate lists — what everyone does because they live here, and optional work that actually pays — plus whatever the person is saving toward. Closer to how a household really runs, and to how money really works.

Why the two lists are kept apart

Contributions are expected and unpaid. Your plate, your laundry, the mess you made. Paying for these teaches that ordinary decency is negotiable, and it stops working the moment the payment does.

Jobs are optional, above and beyond, and they pay — real money, or credit toward something the person chose themselves. Washing the car, sorting the recycling, helping with the shopping.

Adults belong on this board too. A board where only the children have tasks is a compliance chart. One where everybody's contributions are visible is a household — and it quietly answers why do I have to and you don't.

This stays on your device. Nothing is sent anywhere, there is no account, and nobody — including us — can see what you write. It saves in the browser on the device that you are currently using, so it is here tomorrow.

That also means it lives in one browser on one device. If you clear your browsing data, use a different phone, or lose the device, it is gone. Download a backup file every few weeks — see using these tools.

Ticks clear on their own each day. Nothing is scored, nothing is lost, and nobody is compared with anybody.

What are you saving for?

Chosen by the person, not offered by an adult. It can be anything — an object, a trip, a game, a day out. The point is that it is theirs.

When a paid job is done, add it here. Small and often works better than a weekly reckoning.

If something never gets done

Before treating it as a motivation problem, ask whether the person can actually do it — all of it, in the right order, without being reminded of each part.

Very often the answer is no, and the fix is breaking it into steps or doing it alongside them. A task someone cannot yet do is not a discipline question.

There are no points, stars, levels or streaks here, and nothing is ever taken away. Those mechanics work for a while and then stop, and they teach that the task was only ever worth doing for the token.

What does the motivating instead: a goal the person chose, jobs they picked rather than were assigned, adults visibly doing their share, and the plain satisfaction of ticking something off. See choosing behavior support on why this distinction matters.

Reading this as a professional

Deliberately not a token economy. Contributions carry no contingency at all; only optional jobs above the baseline are paid, which mirrors how earning actually works and avoids teaching that participation in a household is transactional.

If a family reports tasks going undone, look first at task analysis and executive demand rather than at motivation — the steps field exists for exactly that conversation.
