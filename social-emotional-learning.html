Social and emotional learning:
the part nobody schedules

Noticing what you feel, managing it, reading other people, handling a relationship, deciding what to do. These are skills, they are teachable, and almost everything else a person is asked to do at school and at work rests on them. They are also the first thing cut when a timetable gets tight.

The abbreviation is SEL, and you will meet it in school documents without anybody explaining it. It covers the same ground families mean by "handling himself" or "getting on with people" — organized so it can actually be taught rather than hoped for.

What it actually covers

Five areas, widely used in schools, and worth knowing by name so you can ask which of them is being taught.

Knowing yourself

Noticing what you feel and what is happening in your body, and having words for it.

  • Recognizing an emotion while it is happening, not afterward
  • Noticing hunger, tiredness, pain, needing the bathroom
  • Knowing what you are good at and what is hard
  • Understanding what tends to set you off

Often missedInteroception — sensing the body's own signals — is genuinely harder for some people, and everything above depends on it.

Managing yourself

Doing something useful with what you have noticed.

  • Waiting, stopping, starting
  • Calming down, and knowing what helps you specifically
  • Asking for help before it becomes a crisis
  • Keeping going when something is hard

NoteThis is not compliance. A person who can regulate can also refuse clearly, which is a skill rather than a problem.

Understanding others

Working out what somebody else might be feeling or thinking.

  • Reading faces, tone and situations — with the caveat below
  • Recognizing that other people want different things
  • Noticing when somebody needs help
  • Understanding that difference is normal

The caveatThis is taught as though one way of reading people is correct. It is a two-way street — see below.

Relationships

The mechanics of being with other people over time.

  • Starting a conversation, and ending one
  • Joining something already happening
  • Disagreeing without it ending the friendship
  • Repairing something after it goes wrong
  • Recognizing when a relationship is not safe

UnderratedRepair is the skill that keeps friendships. It is rarely taught explicitly, and it can be.

Deciding

Working out what to do, and living with it.

  • Thinking past the next thirty seconds
  • Weighing what happens if
  • Knowing what matters to you
  • Taking responsibility without collapsing into shame

Worth knowingPoor decisions under stress are a capacity problem far more often than a values problem.

Why it is not optional

What rests on top of all of it.

  • Learning — a dysregulated brain does not take information in
  • Friendship, which is protective across a whole life
  • Employment, where these skills matter more than the technical ones
  • Safety — refusing, reporting, recognizing when something is wrong
  • Mental health, in both directions

The short versionNearly everything else is downstream of this.

Why it matters more for some people

And why the usual way of teaching it sometimes makes things worse.

  • Much of it is normally absorbed rather than taught. Most children pick up a great deal of this by watching, without anybody planning it. A person who does not absorb it that way is not failing — they need it made explicit, and explicit teaching works.
  • Interoception underlies the whole thing. If you cannot reliably feel hunger, tiredness or the early rise of anger, you cannot act on it early. That is not a lack of self-control; it is missing the signal. Teaching body awareness comes before teaching strategies.
  • Regulation is learned through co-regulation. A person becomes able to settle themselves by being settled with, many times, by somebody calm. Which means the adult's state is part of the curriculum, and a program delivered by a stressed adult in a chaotic room teaches something other than what it says.
  • Capacity comes first. None of these skills are available to somebody who is past their limit. Teaching them in the moment does not work — see the whole picture and in the moment.
  • Skills do not travel on their own. Something learned in a small group at ten on a Tuesday will not appear in the playground unless it is deliberately practiced there. See the goals tracker on generalization.

Where SEL goes wrong for disabled and neurodivergent people

Worth naming, because this is a genuine and reasonable criticism.

  • It can become compliance training wearing better language. If "self-management" in practice means sitting still and not objecting, it is not teaching regulation — it is teaching that your own signals should be overridden. That is the opposite of the skill.
  • Social skills are often taught in one direction only. An autistic person is taught to make eye contact, read tone, and mask discomfort, while nobody teaches their peers to accommodate a different style. Communication difficulty between two people is shared, and putting the whole burden on one of them is both unfair and less effective.
  • Masking is not a skill, and it has a cost. Programs that reward looking regulated rather than being regulated produce people who suppress distress until it arrives all at once, often at home, often years later.
  • Emotion charts assume interoception. A person who cannot feel the difference between anxious and hungry cannot point at the right face, and gets marked as not engaging.
  • Scripted programs assume a shared baseline. Off-the-shelf curricula frequently assume speech, stable attention, and typical sensory experience. They are worth adapting rather than abandoning.

The test: ask whether the goal is for the person to be more comfortable and more effective on their own terms, or for them to be less inconvenient to the adults. Both get described the same way, and the difference is the whole thing.

What good looks like

At home, without a curriculum

  • Name feelings out loud, including your own. "I am frustrated, so I am going to sit down for a minute." Modeling does most of the teaching.
  • Build the vocabulary. Beyond happy, sad and angry — disappointed, overwhelmed, embarrassed, relieved, left out. Precision helps enormously, because a feeling you can name is one you can do something about.
  • Connect the body to the feeling. "Your shoulders came up. What is happening in your tummy?"
  • Repair visibly when you get it wrong. Children learn repair by seeing adults do it, and almost nothing else teaches it as well.
  • Let them see you regulate. Not perfectly — out loud. "That was a lot. I am going outside for two minutes."
  • Debrief afterward, never during. Nothing is learnable in the middle of it. Later, gently, once.
  • Practice in the real place. Rehearse in the car before the shop, not only in the calm of the kitchen — see the teach-back.

At school

  • Taught everywhere, not in one lesson. A weekly session that nothing else reinforces will not transfer.
  • Every adult using the same language, including in the hallway and the cafeteria.
  • Explicit teaching for those who need it, with practice in the real setting.
  • Peers taught too. A school working on inclusion should be teaching everybody how to include, not only teaching disabled students how to fit.
  • Goals that describe the person's own benefit — "will ask for a break when he notices he is getting overwhelmed" rather than "will remain seated".
  • It belongs in the IEP where it is a need. Social and emotional goals are legitimate IEP goals — see inside the IEP.

The question fair to ask about any program

Schools use a range of named curricula, and they vary. Rather than evaluating the brand, ask:

Does it teach noticing before strategies? Strategies without body awareness do not get used.

Is it practiced where it is needed, or only where it is taught?

Does it accept a genuine no? A program in which the right answer is always to continue is teaching compliance.

Is anybody teaching the other children anything?

And would my person recognize themselves in it — or is it built for somebody else and applied to them?

Before you close this

If you do one thing from this page, make it naming feelings out loud — yours as much as theirs. It costs nothing, it needs no program, and it is doing more of the work than any curriculum.

And if a program is teaching your person to be easier for adults rather than more comfortable in themselves, you are allowed to say so.

Nothing here is clinical or educational advice. Where emotional difficulty is significant or persistent, it deserves proper assessment rather than a curriculum.
