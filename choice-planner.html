The choice planner:

Work out what you will say before you are in the moment, so that you are calm, the wording is consistent, and everyone in the house — including a grandparent or a new carer — says the same sentence. Not a reward system. Closer to how the world actually works.

Three different things, often blurred together

Knowing which one you are using keeps it honest — and stops the third one turning into a punishment wearing choice language.

A logical consequence

The thing being misused is the thing that stops. Directly connected, easy to explain, and the version that holds up best when someone is upset.

"Sending it down the stairs isn't using it the right way. You can choose to use it properly and keep going, or you can choose to be all done with it for today — and that's okay, we can try again tomorrow."

First this, then that

An expectation comes before a preferred thing. The two are not inherently connected, so this one relies on the expectation being fair and stated in advance.

"Cleaning up is what happens after an activity. I'll help — I'm being flexible, and I need you to be flexible too. If you choose not to help, you're choosing not to have the iPad after."

A natural consequence

Nobody imposes anything; the world simply does what it does. The strongest teacher available, and the one adults most often interrupt.

"If you don't wear the coat you'll be cold" — and then letting them be a bit cold, where it is safe to do so.

Before any of it — the question that matters most

Can they actually do this, right now? Not in principle, not on a good day. In this moment, with the capacity they have left.

If the answer is no, then "you're choosing" is not true — they are not choosing, they cannot. Saying it anyway teaches a person that their genuine limits are treated as defiance, and that is the single most damaging thing this approach can do.

When capacity is the problem, the move is to lower the demand, help more, or come back to it. That is not letting them off. It is responding to what is actually happening.

What makes this different from a reward system

  • The consequence is connected to the thing, wherever possible, rather than being an unrelated loss applied as a lesson.
  • It is said in advance, calmly, before anyone is upset — not announced in the middle of a hard moment.
  • The door reopens. "We can try again tomorrow" is the part that makes it a boundary rather than a punishment.
  • The tone does not change. No disappointment, no lecture, no I-told-you-so afterward.
  • It is honoured exactly. A consequence stated and not followed through teaches that words are negotiable; one that shifts with your mood teaches that the rule is really about you.
  • Nothing already earned is taken away. Removing something a person already has is a different act, and it is the one that damages trust.

Where this is the wrong tool

Choice-and-consequence language assumes a person can weigh two options and act on the answer. It does not fit a person who is already dysregulated, and it does not fit demand avoidance — where the demand itself is the trigger, and offering a choice inside a demand is still a demand.

In both cases, see in the moment instead. Save this for the calm, ordinary, everyday version.

Who this is for

If you find yourself using these constantly, something else is going on. A household needing a script for everything usually has a capacity problem, a communication problem, or a demand load that is simply too high — none of which is solved by better wording.

The whole picture covers the stacking that puts someone past capacity, and behavior is communication covers looking underneath before responding.

Reading this as a professional

Families arrive using choice-and-consequence language with wildly varying degrees of consistency, and the failure is almost always in follow-through or in capacity mismatch rather than in the wording.

The capacity field is the one worth reading. A script marked "not reliably" is a demand that needs reducing or teaching, not a consequence that needs enforcing.

Nothing here is clinical advice. Where behavior is frequent, escalating, or unsafe, work with someone who knows the person rather than adjusting scripts alone.
